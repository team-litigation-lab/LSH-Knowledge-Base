*A guide for case managers, medical summary writers and demand staff. There is no formula in the law for pain and suffering, and the rules differ by state and by court. Use this as a framework to understand how a number is built and what to gather to support it, and confirm every figure and every legal rule with the handling attorney. The attorney sets the value of a case, not the support team.*

## 1. What non-economic damages are

When a person is hurt because of someone else's carelessness, the law lets them recover **damages**: money to make up for the harm. Damages fall into two main groups.

| | **Economic damages (special damages)** | **Non-economic damages (general damages)** |
|---|---|---|
| **What they cover** | Losses with a bill, a pay stub or a receipt | Losses that have no price tag, because they are about how the injury changed the person's life |
| **Examples** | Medical bills, future medical care, lost wages, lost earning capacity, property damage, out-of-pocket costs | Pain and suffering, mental anguish, loss of enjoyment of life, scarring, physical impairment, loss of companionship |
| **How the amount is found** | Add up the documents | Judgment, supported by evidence |
| **Typical proof** | Bills, records, wage statements, estimates | Medical records, photos, the client's own account, statements from family, friends and co-workers |

**Punitive damages** are a third, separate category. They punish especially bad conduct and are rare in ordinary injury claims, so they are not part of this page.

### The main kinds of non-economic loss

| Kind of loss | What it means in real life |
|---|---|
| **Pain and suffering** | Physical pain, soreness, stiffness, headaches, the discomfort of treatment, injections and surgery, and the pain that continues afterwards. |
| **Mental anguish and emotional distress** | Fear, anxiety, depression, trouble sleeping, flashbacks, fear of driving, and the stress of a long recovery. |
| **Loss of enjoyment of life** (sometimes called hedonic damages) | Not being able to do the things that make up a good life: playing with the kids, sports, gardening, travel, church, hobbies, even a full night's sleep. |
| **Disfigurement and scarring** | Visible scars, burns or changes to appearance, and the embarrassment that comes with them. |
| **Physical impairment or disability** | Permanent loss of movement, strength or function, and the limits it puts on everyday tasks. |
| **Loss of consortium** | A spouse's own claim for the loss of companionship, help and intimacy caused by the partner's injury. |
| **Inconvenience and humiliation** | Needing help with bathing or dressing, giving up independence, and the indignity of it. |

Non-economic damages are usually described in two parts: **past** (from the accident to today, or to trial) and **future** (for the rest of the person's life, if the injury is permanent). A demand often values the two separately.

## 2. Why there is no exact number, and what the support team's job is

A bill has an amount on it. Pain does not. A jury, or an adjuster guessing what a jury would do, has to turn the effect of an injury on a person's life into dollars. Courts do not give a formula for this, so the methods below are **tools for negotiation and for explaining a figure**. They are not rules of law.

For the support team, the job is **not** to put a value on the case. The job is to:

- **Gather and organize** the evidence that shows how the injury affected the person: medical records, bills, photos, statements.
- **Build the medical chronology** so the attorney can see the pain, the treatment and the recovery in order.
- **Check the numbers**: totals, dates, duplicates, unpaid bills, anything that does not belong to this accident.
- **Flag risks**: treatment gaps, a prior injury to the same body part, inconsistent histories, a long wait before the first visit.
- **Draft what the attorney asks for**, such as the damages section of the demand, and let the attorney decide the figure.

**Never** quote a case value, a range or a "typical" settlement to a client, an adjuster or anyone else unless the attorney has told you to. Clients remember numbers.

## 3. How a value is built, step by step

1. **Verify the economic damages.** Add up the medical bills, lost wages and other costs, using only items related to this accident. This is the base for most methods.
2. **Understand the injury.** What was injured, how badly, and how long did it last? Was there surgery, injections, a hospital stay, a fracture, a head injury, a scar, a permanent restriction?
3. **Check permanency.** Did a doctor say the problem is permanent, give an impairment rating or describe future care? This is often the biggest single driver of value.
4. **Pick the method (or two).** The attorney chooses. Running two methods and comparing is common, so one number does not stand on its own.
5. **Adjust for the case itself.** Strength of liability, the client's share of fault, the venue, the policy limits and whether the at-fault party can pay.
6. **Set a range.** Attorneys usually think in a range: the lowest figure the client would accept, a target, and an opening demand.
7. **Write the reasoning down.** In the demand, the figure is supported by a story and evidence. A number without a reason is easy to dismiss.

## 4. The common methods

### 4.1 The multiplier method

The medical bills (and sometimes lost wages as well) are multiplied by a number, often between about **1.5 and 5**. The multiplier is higher when the injury is more serious, permanent, supported by objective findings and the other side clearly caused the accident. It is lower when treatment was short, the injury healed, or there are problems with the evidence.

**Example A: a neck and back injury that healed after four months.**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Medical bills | $12,000 |
| Lost wages | $2,500 |
| Multiplier on medical bills | 2.5 |
| **Non-economic damages** ($12,000 × 2.5) | **$30,000** |
| **Total** ($12,000 + $2,500 + $30,000) | **$44,500** |

If the attorney applies the multiplier to bills **and** wages together, the result is: ($12,000 + $2,500) × 2.5 = $36,250 non-economic, and a total of $14,500 + $36,250 = **$50,750**.

**Example B: a broken leg that needed surgery, with a lasting limp.**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Medical bills | $85,000 |
| Lost wages | $20,000 |
| Multiplier on medical bills | 4 |
| **Non-economic damages** ($85,000 × 4) | **$340,000** |
| **Total** ($85,000 + $20,000 + $340,000) | **$445,000** |

The total is a starting point for negotiation. The most the insurer can pay is limited by the policy limits, and the client's own share of fault, if any, reduces it.

**Watch out for:**

- The multiplier is a **rule of thumb**, not a law, and insurers do not all use it.
- It rewards large medical bills. A serious injury with little treatment (for example, someone who could not afford care) looks smaller under this method than it is.
- In some states only the amount actually **paid** or owed for treatment can be shown to a jury, not the full amount billed. That changes the base the multiplier is applied to.

### 4.2 The per diem method

"Per diem" means "per day". A dollar amount is given to each day of pain and multiplied by the number of days.

**Example: the same neck and back injury.**

| Period | Rate | Days | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| Active treatment | $120 a day | 150 | $18,000 |
| Lingering pain after treatment ended | $40 a day | 365 | $14,600 |
| **Total (past)** | | | **$32,600** |

For a **permanent** injury the per diem can be extended over the person's **life expectancy**, taken from published life tables. For example, $25 a day for 20 years is $25 × 365 × 20 = **$182,500**. Whether future amounts are reduced to their present value varies by state.

**Watch out for:**

- Choosing the daily rate is the hard part. Some attorneys tie it to something a jury can relate to, such as what a fair day's pay would be for putting up with the pain.
- Some courts do not let a lawyer suggest a per diem figure to a jury. The attorney will know the local rule.
- It works best when the period of pain is clear. It is a poor fit if the dates of treatment are unclear or there are long gaps.

### 4.3 Claims evaluation software

Many insurance companies run injury claims through **evaluation software** (Colossus is a well-known one). The adjuster enters the type of injury, the treatment, the length of care, the effect on work and other facts, and the program suggests a range. This is why the **way an injury is described and coded in the medical records matters**: specific diagnoses, objective findings and consistent notes tend to score higher than general complaints.

### 4.4 Comparable verdicts and settlements

Attorneys compare the case with **similar injuries** that ended in verdicts or settlements in the same area. Age, injury, treatment, permanency and venue all matter. This is research and judgment, and it is the method most likely to reflect what a jury in that court would actually do.

### 4.5 Quick comparison

| Method | How it works | Best for | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Multiplier** | Medical bills × a number | Everyday injury claims with documented treatment | Depends on the size of the bills; the multiplier is a guess |
| **Per diem** | Daily rate × days of pain | Injuries with a clear start and end, and permanent injuries over a lifetime | The daily rate is arbitrary; some courts do not allow it |
| **Evaluation software** | Points for injury, treatment, and other inputs | Understanding what an insurer is likely to offer | Depends on how well the file is documented |
| **Comparable verdicts** | Similar cases in the same area | Serious or unusual cases | Hard to find truly similar cases |

## 5. What raises or lowers the value

| Tends to raise the value | Tends to lower the value |
|---|---|
| Surgery, fractures, a head or spinal injury, scarring | A minor injury that resolved quickly |
| A doctor's opinion that the injury is permanent, with an impairment rating | No permanency opinion |
| Objective findings: MRI or CT results, x-rays, test results | Complaints with no objective findings |
| Consistent care from the first visit onward | A long delay before the first visit, or gaps in treatment |
| Clear effect on work, sleep, family life and hobbies, shown by evidence | Little or no evidence of how daily life changed |
| A sympathetic, credible client with a clean history | Inconsistent statements, social media posts that contradict the claim |
| Clear fault on the other side, strong evidence of how it happened | Disputed fault, or a share of fault on the client |
| High policy limits, or a defendant who can pay | Low limits and no other source of recovery |
| A venue that tends to be generous to injured people | A venue that tends to be conservative |
| Younger age at injury (a longer life with the injury) | Prior injuries or conditions in the same body part (the other side will blame them) |

Two of these deserve a closer look.

**Treatment gaps.** A gap in care lets the insurer argue the person got better, or that the pain was not serious. If a gap exists, find out why (cost, work, family, a provider closing, the person was waiting on an approval) and make sure it is explained in the file. See the page on handling treatment gaps in the demand phase.

**Pre-existing conditions.** A prior injury or condition does not end the claim. The law generally lets a person recover for an **aggravation** of a prior condition, but the demand has to show what the person was like **before** and what is different **now**. Collect the older records so the comparison is based on facts rather than guesses.

## 6. Limits and special rules that change the number

These vary a great deal by state. Check with the attorney on the rule that applies to the case.

- **Comparative fault.** If the client is partly at fault, the total award, including non-economic damages, is reduced by the client's percentage. Some states bar recovery if the client is more than half at fault, and a few bar it if the client is even slightly at fault.
- **No-fault thresholds.** In a no-fault state, the right to claim pain and suffering from the at-fault driver in a car accident is limited unless the injury meets a **threshold**. Florida is a common example: the injury generally has to be a permanent injury (or involve significant scarring or disfigurement, or death) before non-economic damages can be claimed from the at-fault driver. Other states use terms such as "serious injury" or "serious impairment". In these states, **a doctor's opinion on permanency is central to the case**.
- **Caps.** Some states cap non-economic damages in certain kinds of cases (medical malpractice and claims against the government are the most common), and a few have wider caps. Caps are state-specific, and some have been struck down by courts.
- **Policy limits.** A claim can be worth more than the money available. Check the limits early.
- **Liens and reductions.** What the client takes home is affected by liens and fees, but those do not change the value of the claim itself. Keep them separate when you write about damages.

## 7. What to gather to support non-economic damages

This is the support team's checklist. The more specific and the more dated the evidence is, the better it supports the number.

**Medical**

- [ ] Complete medical records and bills from every provider
- [ ] Imaging reports and, where requested, the images
- [ ] A medical chronology or summary showing dates, complaints, pain levels, treatment and progress
- [ ] A doctor's report or narrative on **permanency, impairment rating, work and activity restrictions and future care**
- [ ] A future care estimate, such as a recommended surgery or ongoing therapy, with the expected cost
- [ ] Mental health treatment records, if any, and records of medication for pain, anxiety or sleep

**Photos and records of the injury**

- [ ] Photos of injuries at the start, during healing and now, including scars, bruises, casts and braces
- [ ] Photos of the vehicles or the scene, if they show the force of the impact

**The person's own account**

- [ ] A pain journal or diary: what hurts, how much, and what the person could not do that day
- [ ] The client's own description of life before and after the accident

**Statements from other people**

- [ ] Short statements from family, friends, co-workers or a supervisor describing the person before and after
- [ ] An employer letter on missed work, light duty or a change in duties
- [ ] Records of activities given up: team rosters, gym or class memberships, travel plans cancelled

**The baseline**

- [ ] Earlier records for the same body part, so the "before" is accurate

## 8. The non-economic damages in the demand

The damages section of the demand usually does three things:

1. **Lists the economic damages** with exact totals and where each number comes from.
2. **Tells the story of the non-economic harm** in specific, human detail: the date of the accident, what the first weeks were like, what the person could no longer do, what the doctors said about the future. "The client could not lift her two-year-old for six weeks" does more than "the client experienced pain and suffering".
3. **States the demand figure**, as the attorney decides, with the reasoning behind it.

Keep the tone factual. Avoid exaggeration, because anything an adjuster can disprove weakens the rest of the demand. See also the pages in this library on writing a demand letter and on the pre-demand requirements.

## 9. Common mistakes

- **Treating a method as the law.** A multiplier or per diem is a way of explaining a figure. Do not present it as the rule.
- **Quoting a number to the client** or to the adjuster without the attorney's approval.
- **Including bills that do not belong**: unrelated treatment, duplicates, or charges from before the accident.
- **Missing the permanency opinion.** In many states this decides whether non-economic damages can be claimed at all.
- **Ignoring treatment gaps** instead of explaining them.
- **Thin evidence of daily life.** A demand with ten pages of bills and no description of how the person's life changed leaves money on the table.
- **Counting the same loss twice**, such as lost wages inside a pain and suffering figure.
- **Mixing in liens, fees and take-home amounts** when describing damages.
- **Forgetting future care and future loss** for an injury that is expected to last.
