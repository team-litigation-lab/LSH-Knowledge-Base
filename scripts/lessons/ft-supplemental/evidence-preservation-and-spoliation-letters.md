## Evidence has an expiry date

A personal injury case is built on evidence: photos of the scene, the damaged cars, a store's security video, the other truck's driving logs, the 911 call. Some of that evidence lasts for years. A lot of it does not. Security cameras record over themselves, cars get repaired or crushed, and companies delete records on a schedule. If no one asks for the evidence to be kept, it is often gone long before anyone thinks to request it in a lawsuit.

That is why the firm moves quickly in the first days of a case to **identify** the evidence, **ask the people who hold it to preserve it**, and **collect** what it can. The claims and intake teams are at the front of that work: on Days 10–12 you open claims, send letters of representation and request reports, and the preservation letter is part of the same early push.

This page covers:

- what evidence disappears and how fast,
- what spoliation and preservation letters are,
- what a VA prepares and sends under the attorney's direction,
- photos, witnesses and reports,
- basic chain of custody,
- how to track it all in the case file.

## What evidence disappears, and how fast

The times below are typical, not fixed rules. Retention policies vary from company to company and agency to agency, which is exactly why the firm asks early rather than assumes.

| Evidence | Who holds it | How fast it can disappear |
|---|---|---|
| **Surveillance video** (store, gas station, apartment complex, traffic camera, doorbell camera) | The business, property owner, city or homeowner | Often **days to a few weeks**: many systems record over themselves automatically |
| **The vehicles** themselves | The owners, insurers, body shops, tow yards, salvage auctions | A total-loss car can be sold at salvage within **weeks**; a repaired car loses the evidence of the damage |
| **Event data recorder (EDR) / "black box"** data (speed, braking, seatbelt use just before the crash) | Stored in the vehicle | Lost when the car is repaired, crushed or, in some cases, overwritten by later events |
| **Truck data**: electronic logging device (ELD) hours-of-service data, GPS, dispatch records, dashcams, driver qualification and maintenance files | The trucking company | Some records only have to be kept for a limited time (hours-of-service records are generally kept around **six months** under federal rules), and dashcam footage may be overwritten quickly |
| **Rideshare data** (trip logs, app status, GPS) | Uber, Lyft and similar | Kept under the company's policies; must be requested |
| **Dashcam footage** | The client, the other driver, witnesses, fleet companies | Often overwritten in a loop within hours or days |
| **911 recordings and dispatch (CAD) logs** | The local emergency communications agency | Retention varies; some agencies keep them for only **weeks or months** |
| **Store incident reports**, cleaning and inspection logs, employee statements | The business (often its insurer or third-party claims administrator) | Kept under internal policies; may be hard to get later |
| **The scene itself** (the spill, the broken step, the pothole, the missing sign) | The property owner or government | Can be fixed or changed **the same day** |
| **Phone records** (to prove texting while driving) | The carriers | Kept under the carrier's policies; usually obtained in litigation |
| **The client's own evidence**: photos, clothing, shoes, broken glasses, a damaged helmet or car seat | The client | Thrown away, washed, repaired or lost |

## Spoliation: what happens when evidence is destroyed

**Spoliation** is the destruction, loss or significant alteration of evidence when a party knew, or should have known, that it was relevant to a likely lawsuit.

When a court finds spoliation, it can impose consequences, depending on the state and how serious it was, for example:

- telling the jury it may assume the missing evidence would have hurt the party that lost it (an **adverse inference**),
- excluding other evidence or testimony,
- fines or, in serious cases, judgment against the party.

But these remedies usually depend on showing that the other side **was on notice** that it should keep the evidence. That is the point of a preservation letter: it puts the holder **on notice in writing**, on a date the firm can prove.

The duty goes both ways. **The client must preserve evidence too.** If the client repairs the car, deletes photos or posts and deletes social media content, the other side can raise spoliation against the client. The attorney instructs the client on this; you may be the one who passes that instruction on at the welcome call.

## Preservation letters (spoliation letters, litigation hold letters)

A **preservation letter** (also called a spoliation letter, an evidence preservation letter or, when sent to a party's own organization, a litigation hold notice) is a formal written request to someone who holds evidence, asking them to **keep it and not destroy, alter or overwrite it**.

### What it usually contains

- The firm's letterhead, the date and the attorney's signature block.
- That the firm represents the client in a claim arising from an incident (date of loss, location).
- A clear list of the evidence to preserve, as specific as possible (for example, "all video from all cameras at the store at 123 Main St. from 2:00 p.m. to 4:00 p.m. on [date]," or "the 2022 Freightliner, VIN or unit number ___, and its EDR, ECM and ELD data").
- That the request includes **electronically stored information** (video, data, emails, texts, app data).
- A request that the vehicle or object not be repaired, sold or destroyed without notice and an opportunity to inspect.
- A request to forward the letter to the holder's insurer, and the firm's contact details.

### Who it goes to

Under the attorney's direction, often:

- the at-fault party and their **insurer** (both, when known),
- the **business or property owner** where the incident happened (and its management company),
- the at-fault driver's **employer** or trucking company,
- **tow yards, body shops and salvage auctions** holding a vehicle,
- the **rideshare company**,
- **government agencies** for traffic camera video or 911 recordings (these often need a public-records request instead; the attorney says which),
- **the client** (a written instruction to keep their own evidence).

### What the VA does, and doesn't do

The attorney decides **whether** a letter is sent, **to whom** and **what it asks for**. Your work, when assigned:

1. **Prepare the letter** from the firm's approved template, filling in the case details exactly (names, DOL, location, vehicle details, camera locations, time window).
2. **Have the attorney review and sign** it. Never send a preservation letter that has not been approved.
3. **Send it the way the firm requires**: often more than one method (email, fax, certified mail with return receipt) so the firm can prove it was received.
4. **Save** the sent letter, the proof of sending (fax confirmation, certified mail receipt, email) and any reply in the case file, named by the firm's convention.
5. **Calendar follow-ups**: a reminder to check for a response, and to send the evidence request (or a records request) once the holder confirms it has the evidence.
6. **Note it** in the case notes: what was sent, to whom, how and when.

Speed matters most with video. If the intake notes mention cameras at a store or a gas station, tell your lead the same day so the attorney can act before the footage is overwritten.

## Photographs: what to ask the client for

At intake and at the welcome call, ask the client to send everything they have, as **original files** (not screenshots, and not edited or filtered), so the date and time information in the file is kept. A useful checklist:

- **The scene**: wide shots, the intersection, traffic lights and signs, skid marks, debris, the hazard (spill, hole, broken step), lighting, weather.
- **All vehicles**: every side, close-ups of damage, the license plates, the inside (airbags, seats, child seats).
- **Injuries**: on the day and over time (bruising changes; photos taken every few days show healing and severity), stitches, casts, surgical scars, medical devices.
- **The client's property**: torn clothing, shoes worn in a fall, broken glasses, a damaged phone or helmet.
- **Documents at the scene**: the other driver's license and insurance card, the officer's card or report number.

Tell the client, as the firm instructs: **keep the originals**, don't delete anything, don't post photos or comments about the accident on social media, and don't repair or dispose of anything without checking with the firm first.

## Witnesses

Independent witnesses (people who were not in either car and don't know either side) are some of the strongest evidence of fault, and they are hard to find later. Capture:

- full name, phone number, email address and city,
- where they were and what they saw (in the client's words or the witness's, as reported),
- whether they spoke to the police or are named in the report,
- whether they took photos or video.

Do not interview a witness yourself unless the attorney tells you to and gives you the questions. Pass the details on.

## Police and crash reports, and other records

On Days 10–12 you request reports as part of opening the claim:

- **Police / crash report**: request it from the agency that responded (city police, county sheriff, state highway patrol) or through the state's online crash report system. Note the report number, the agency and the officer.
- **911 recordings and CAD logs**, if the attorney asks: usually through a public-records request to the agency. Send it quickly because of short retention.
- **Fire/EMS run reports**: the ambulance or fire rescue report, which shows the client's complaints at the scene. (The Records team also requests these with the medical records, Days 13–16.)
- **Incident reports** from a store or property: the business usually won't hand these over before a lawsuit, but the request and the preservation letter put it on notice.
- **Weather and traffic data**, if the attorney needs them.

Some states restrict who can get a crash report for a period after the crash (to the parties, their lawyers and insurers). Follow the firm's procedure and identify the firm as the client's representative.

## Chain of custody, the basics

**Chain of custody** is the record of who had a piece of evidence, from when to when, so that no one can argue it was changed. For a firm, that mostly means:

- **Keep originals as originals.** Save the file the client sent; work from a copy.
- **Record where it came from**: who sent it, when, how (email, upload, USB) and in what form.
- **Don't edit, crop, rename over or convert** the original file. If a converted or trimmed copy is needed, save it as a separate file and say so in its name.
- **Physical evidence** (a helmet, a shoe, a car seat) is kept by the client or stored as the attorney directs, labelled and logged. Don't let anyone clean, repair or test it without the attorney's instruction.
- **Note every hand-off** in the case notes.

## Tracking it in the case file

Keep an evidence list (or use the case system's evidence or tasks section, depending on the firm) with, for each item:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Item | Store video, 2 p.m.–4 p.m., all cameras |
| Held by | ABC Grocery, Store #114 (manager: ___) |
| Preservation letter | Sent 05/14 by email and certified mail; receipt saved |
| Response | Insurer confirmed video saved, 05/20 |
| Obtained? | Not yet; request after suit, per attorney |
| Next follow-up | 06/04 |

The point is that anyone on the team can open the file and see, at a glance, what evidence exists, who has it, whether it is protected and what happens next.

## What you must never do

- **Never send a preservation letter** (or any letter on firm letterhead) without the attorney's approval and signature.
- **Never tell the client or a caller that evidence "proves" the case** or who was at fault.
- **Never tell the client to delete, edit or throw away** anything, including social media posts. Questions about social media go to the attorney.
- **Never contact a represented party directly** (for example, the at-fault driver if they have a lawyer). Letters go to their insurer or counsel as the attorney directs.
- **Never edit or overwrite** an original photo or video file.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Waiting to ask about cameras until the demand stage | The video was overwritten weeks ago | Ask at intake; flag cameras the same day |
| A vague letter ("please keep all evidence") | The holder can say it didn't know what to keep | List specific items, locations and time windows |
| Sending by one method with no proof | The other side can deny it received the letter | Use the firm's methods and save every confirmation |
| Saving screenshots instead of original photos | Date and time information is lost | Ask for originals; save them untouched |
| Telling the client "it's fine to fix the car" | The car and its data are lost as evidence | Pass the question to the attorney |
| No follow-up after sending | No one knows whether the evidence was kept | Calendar the follow-up and note the response |

## Quick reference

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Spoliation** | Destroying, losing or altering evidence relevant to a likely lawsuit |
| **Preservation letter** | A written request to keep evidence; puts the holder on notice |
| **Litigation hold** | An organization's internal order to stop deleting relevant records |
| **Adverse inference** | A jury may assume lost evidence would have hurt the party that lost it |
| **ESI** | Electronically stored information: video, data, emails, texts, app data |
| **EDR** | Event data recorder ("black box") in a vehicle |
| **ELD** | Electronic logging device: a truck's hours-of-service record |
| **CAD log** | The 911 dispatch record |
| **Chain of custody** | The record of who had a piece of evidence and when |

**The rules for staff:** spot the evidence early (cameras, vehicles, trucks, witnesses) · flag fast-disappearing evidence the same day · prepare letters from the template for the attorney's approval · send with proof, save everything, calendar the follow-up · keep originals untouched · never tell the client to delete or repair anything.
