## Liens in personal injury cases: who gets paid back, the law behind it and the VA's part

When a personal injury case settles, the check doesn't belong only to the client. Health insurers, government programs, hospitals and treating providers may have a legal right to be **paid back out of the settlement** for what they paid or are owed. Those rights are **liens** (or reimbursement and subrogation claims). If a lien is missed or paid wrong, the client, the firm and even the attorney personally can be on the hook.

Days 17–18 (Lien Negotiator Training) teach the lien negotiator's workflow. This page gives you the background: what each kind of lien is, the law that creates it, how it is resolved and what you do on each one.

## 1. Lien, subrogation, reimbursement: the words

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Lien** | A legal claim against money or property (here, the settlement) to secure a debt |
| **Subrogation** | The right of a payer (for example the client's health insurer) to "step into the client's shoes" and recover what it paid from the party who caused the injury |
| **Reimbursement** | The right to be paid back directly out of the client's recovery |
| **Lienholder** | Whoever claims the lien: Medicare, a health plan, a hospital, a provider |
| **Conditional payment** | Medicare's term for paying a bill that another party (the liability insurer) should ultimately pay |
| **Itemization** | The lienholder's list of the charges it paid, by provider and date |
| **Final lien / final demand / payoff** | The final amount the lienholder will accept to release its claim |
| **Reduction** | A lower payoff the attorney negotiates (for procurement costs, hardship, limited recovery, unrelated charges) |
| **Disbursement** | Paying out the settlement: lienholders, providers, fees and costs, then the client |

## 2. Medicare

### The law

The **Medicare Secondary Payer Act (MSP)**, **42 U.S.C. § 1395y(b)**, makes Medicare a *secondary* payer when liability insurance, no-fault (PIP/MedPay) or workers' compensation is responsible. When Medicare pays accident-related bills anyway, those are **conditional payments**, and Medicare must be repaid from the settlement.

Medicare's recovery right is strong:

- It can recover from the beneficiary, the insurer **and anyone who received settlement money**, including the law firm.
- The government can seek **double damages** in some cases.
- Liability insurers must report settlements with Medicare beneficiaries to CMS (**Section 111 reporting**), so Medicare will learn of the settlement.
- **Interest** starts to accrue if the final demand isn't paid within the time given (generally 60 days from the demand letter).

### How the process works

1. **Identify** the client as a Medicare beneficiary at intake (age 65+, disability benefits for two years, end-stage renal disease). Ask for the Medicare card (the Medicare Beneficiary Identifier, MBI).
2. **Report the case** to the **Benefits Coordination & Recovery Center (BCRC)**, with the client's consent or proof of representation, by phone, mail or the **Medicare Secondary Payer Recovery Portal (MSPRP)**.
3. Receive the **Conditional Payment Letter (CPL)**, listing the accident-related claims Medicare paid so far.
4. **Review the itemization** for charges unrelated to the accident; the attorney disputes those.
5. Keep it updated; Medicare keeps adding claims while treatment continues.
6. After settlement, report the settlement amount, fees and costs; Medicare issues a **Final Demand**.
7. Medicare applies the **procurement cost reduction** under **42 CFR § 411.37**: it reduces its recovery by its proportionate share of the attorney's fees and costs.
8. Pay the final demand (or appeal / request a waiver or compromise as the attorney decides) and keep proof of payment.

**Medicare Advantage (Part C) plans** are run by private insurers (Humana, UnitedHealthcare and others). They also claim reimbursement, often through recovery vendors, and many courts have held they can enforce MSP rights. Treat a Medicare Advantage claim as seriously as traditional Medicare.

**Future medicals:** in some cases (especially workers' compensation, and some liability settlements) the attorney considers Medicare's interest in future accident-related care, sometimes through a **Medicare Set-Aside (MSA)**. That decision belongs to the attorney.

## 3. Medicaid

### The law

Federal law requires each state Medicaid program to seek recovery from liable third parties, and every state has a **Medicaid recovery statute**. Medicaid's lien is real, but the U.S. Supreme Court has limited it:

- **Arkansas Dept. of Health & Human Services v. Ahlborn (2006):** Medicaid may recover only from the part of the settlement that represents **medical expenses**, not the whole settlement.
- **Wos v. E.M.A. (2013):** a state can't use an arbitrary fixed percentage that ignores the actual allocation.
- **Gallardo v. Marstiller (2022):** the medical-expense portion Medicaid can reach includes **future** medical expenses, not only past ones.

Each state sets its own procedure for how the medical portion is determined (some by formula, some by hearing). Florida, for example, has a statutory formula with a way to challenge it before an administrative judge.

### How the process works

1. Identify Medicaid (or a **Medicaid managed care plan**, which often has its own recovery vendor) at intake.
2. Send notice of the claim and the LOR to the state agency or its recovery contractor.
3. Request the **itemization** of accident-related payments.
4. After settlement, request the final amount; the attorney applies the state's rules and negotiates.
5. Pay and get a written release.

## 4. ERISA and employer health plans

Most people with job-based health insurance are in an **ERISA plan** (Employee Retirement Income Security Act). Whether the plan can enforce its reimbursement terms depends on how it is funded.

| Plan type | What it means | Effect on the lien |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-funded** | The employer pays the claims itself; an insurer only administers | ERISA generally **preempts state anti-subrogation and made-whole laws**, so the **plan document controls** |
| **Fully insured** | The employer buys a policy from an insurer, which pays the claims | State insurance laws (including anti-subrogation or made-whole rules) can apply (FMC Corp. v. Holliday, 1990) |

Key Supreme Court cases:

- **US Airways v. McCutchen (2013):** the **plan's terms control**; if the plan clearly says it gets reimbursed in full without sharing attorney fees, it can enforce that. If the plan is silent, the **common-fund doctrine** (sharing the cost of recovery) can apply.
- **Montanile v. Board of Trustees (2016):** a plan generally can't recover from the client's general assets once the settlement money has been spent on things that can't be traced. This is why firms hold disputed lien money in trust.

**Your job on ERISA claims:** ask for the **plan document and the summary plan description (SPD)**, and confirm whether the plan is self-funded or fully insured (the Form 5500 or the plan administrator can show this). The attorney needs both to evaluate the lien.

Other federal plans have their own rules: **FEHBA** plans for federal employees (their reimbursement terms generally preempt state law, Coventry Health Care v. Nevils, 2017), **TRICARE** for military families and the **VA** (under the Federal Medical Care Recovery Act). Flag any of these to the attorney.

## 5. Private health insurance (not ERISA)

Individual plans, marketplace plans and government-employee plans that aren't ERISA are usually governed by **state law**. States differ a lot:

- Some states **ban or limit** health insurer subrogation in personal injury cases.
- Many apply the **made-whole doctrine**: the insurer can't recover until the client is fully compensated.
- Many require the insurer to **share attorney fees** (common fund).

The policy language and the state's law decide what is owed. Request the policy and the itemization, and let the attorney evaluate.

## 6. Hospital and provider liens

### Hospital lien statutes

Many states have **hospital lien statutes** that give hospitals (and sometimes ambulance services or doctors) a lien on the patient's recovery for emergency or accident care. They usually require the hospital to **give notice or file** the lien in a set way and time, and they often **cap** the amount (for example a percentage of the recovery or "reasonable" charges). Florida's hospital liens, for example, come from county or city ordinances, not one state statute. Check each lien notice for the date, the amount and the statute or ordinance it cites, and give it to the case manager.

### Letters of protection (LOP)

A **letter of protection** is the firm's written promise to a provider that its bill will be paid out of the settlement, in exchange for the provider treating the client without payment up front. It is **a contract, not a statutory lien**, and the provider expects the full bill unless it agrees to a reduction.

- Log every LOP: provider, date sent, who signed.
- Track the balance (see *Reading medical bills and records*).
- Before disbursement, get the **final balance in writing**, including any agreed reduction.

### Other liens and claims

| Lien | Law behind it | What to know |
|---|---|---|
| **Workers' compensation** | State workers' comp statutes | If the client was hurt at work, the comp carrier usually has a lien on a third-party recovery; it often reduces for fees under statute |
| **PIP / MedPay** | Policy terms and state law | In some states the client's own auto carrier can seek reimbursement of what it paid; in others (and in many no-fault states) it can't. Check the policy and state law |
| **Child support** | State law | Some states let child support agencies place liens on or intercept personal injury settlements; flag any notice to the attorney |
| **Other** | Varies | Prior attorney's fee or cost liens, attorney charging liens, crime victim compensation funds, disability insurers, tax liens |

## 7. The lien workflow, step by step

1. **Identify** every possible lienholder at intake and throughout the case: health insurance cards, Medicare/Medicaid, how bills were paid, hospital lien notices, LOPs, workers' comp. Ask the client directly.
2. **Notify** each lienholder: send the LOR or notice of claim as the firm's procedure says.
3. **Request itemizations** and plan documents (ERISA) early, not at settlement.
4. **Track** in the case system: lienholder, type, reference/claim number, contact, date notified, last itemization amount, date requested, follow-up date.
5. **Review** itemizations for unrelated charges and send them to the case manager.
6. **Update** amounts periodically and before the demand.
7. **At settlement**, request **final** amounts in writing.
8. **Reductions:** the attorney or lien negotiator negotiates; you prepare the documents (settlement amount, fees and costs, the lien's itemization, hardship information) as instructed.
9. **Disburse** only with final, written amounts; send payment with the lienholder's reference number.
10. **Close out:** get and save the **release or satisfaction letter** for every lien.

## 8. The golden rule

**Never disburse settlement money to the client until every lien has a final written amount, and the money for it is held or paid.** A lien paid from a guess, or ignored, can come back on the client and the firm, sometimes with interest and penalties. When a lien amount is in dispute, the attorney may hold the disputed amount in the trust account until it is resolved.

## 9. Your role and its limits

**You do:** identify lienholders, send notices, request itemizations and plan documents, track and follow up, flag unrelated charges, request final amounts, prepare reduction packets as instructed, and save releases.

**You don't:** decide whether a lien is valid, how much is owed, whether state law or ERISA applies, or negotiate a reduction without the attorney's direction. Never tell a client that a lien "doesn't count" or will be waived.

## Common mistakes

- Not asking at intake whether the client has **Medicare, Medicaid or a Medicare Advantage plan**.
- Waiting until settlement to request itemizations.
- Not asking an employer plan for the **plan document** and whether it is **self-funded**.
- Ignoring a hospital lien notice because "the bill was sent to insurance."
- Treating a provider's $0 balance as no lien when the **health insurer** paid the bill.
- Using an old itemization amount as the final amount.
- Disbursing before getting **final written amounts**.
- Not saving the **release or satisfaction letter** after payment.

## Quick reference

| Lienholder | Law | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| **Medicare** | MSP Act, 42 U.S.C. § 1395y(b) | Must be repaid; CPL → Final Demand; procurement cost reduction, 42 CFR § 411.37; firm can be liable |
| **Medicare Advantage** | MSP Act and plan contract | Treat like Medicare |
| **Medicaid** | State recovery statute + federal law | Only the medical portion (Ahlborn), including future medicals (Gallardo) |
| **ERISA self-funded** | ERISA | Plan document controls (McCutchen); state anti-subrogation law usually doesn't apply |
| **ERISA fully insured / private plan** | State insurance law + policy | Anti-subrogation and made-whole rules may apply |
| **Hospital lien** | State statute or local ordinance | Notice and filing rules; often capped |
| **LOP provider** | Contract | Full bill unless a written reduction |
| **Workers' comp** | State comp statute | Lien on third-party recovery |
| **Child support** | State law | Possible lien or intercept |
