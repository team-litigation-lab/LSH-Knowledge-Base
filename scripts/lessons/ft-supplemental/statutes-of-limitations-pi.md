## Why deadlines are the first thing that can lose a case

A personal injury case can be strong on every other point (clear liability, good insurance, serious injuries) and still be worth nothing if a deadline is missed. Once the time to file a lawsuit runs out, the claim is usually gone for good, and the insurer knows it. No amount of negotiation brings it back.

That is why the firm treats the **date of loss (DOL)** and the deadlines that flow from it as some of the most important facts in the file. You will not decide what any deadline is: that is the attorney's job. But you are often the first person to hear the date, the first to write it down and the first to notice that something about the case (a government vehicle, a child, a death, an old accident) changes the clock. Getting that right, and raising it fast, is part of your job from Day 7 onward.

This page covers:

- what a statute of limitations (SOL) is and when the clock starts,
- what can pause or shorten it,
- the much shorter deadlines for claims against the government,
- insurance policy deadlines that are not lawsuits at all,
- what a VA does (and must never do) with all of this.

## What a statute of limitations is

A **statute of limitations** is a law that sets how long a person has to **file a lawsuit** after they are harmed. If the suit is not filed in court before the deadline, the defendant can ask the court to dismiss it, and the court almost always will.

Three things to understand from the start:

1. **It is a deadline to file suit, not to settle.** Talking to the insurer, opening a claim or sending a demand does **not** stop the clock. If the case has not settled as the deadline gets close, the attorney has to file the lawsuit to protect the claim.
2. **It is set by state law and by the type of claim.** A car accident, a medical malpractice claim, a wrongful death claim and a claim against a city can all have different deadlines, even in the same state.
3. **It can change.** Legislatures change these laws. Florida, for example, shortened its general negligence deadline in 2023 (see the table below). The deadline that applies usually depends on the date of the accident, so an older file and a newer file in the same state can be on different clocks.

## When the clock starts: accrual

The day the clock starts is called the day the claim **accrues**.

| Situation | When it usually starts |
|---|---|
| Car accident, slip and fall, dog bite | On the **date of loss**: the day of the accident or injury |
| An injury the person could not have known about right away (some medical malpractice, toxic exposure) | Often when the person **discovered, or reasonably should have discovered,** the injury and its cause. This is the **discovery rule**. |
| Wrongful death | Often on the **date of death**, which may be later than the date of the accident |
| A claim against the client's own insurer for unpaid benefits | Often tied to the **denial** or the policy's own terms, not the accident |

The discovery rule does not apply to every claim in every state, and it is the attorney who decides whether it applies. Your job is to capture the facts that matter: **when it happened, when the client first noticed symptoms, and when they learned what caused them.**

## What can pause or extend the clock: tolling

**Tolling** means the clock is paused, or does not start, for a period. Whether tolling applies, and for how long, varies a great deal by state. Common examples:

- **Minors.** In many states the clock for a child's own injury claim does not start until the child turns 18. Some states toll only in limited cases, and some put an outside limit on it. **A parent's own claim** (for example, for the child's medical bills) may run on its own, untolled clock. Never assume a child's case has "plenty of time."
- **Mental incapacity.** Some states pause the clock while the injured person is legally incapacitated.
- **The defendant is absent or hiding.** Some states pause the clock while the defendant is out of the state or conceals themselves.
- **Fraud or concealment.** If the defendant hid the wrongdoing, some states delay the start of the clock.
- **Bankruptcy or the death of a party** can also affect deadlines.

Tolling is an exception, argued by lawyers, and courts read it narrowly. The safe working rule for staff: **always calendar the shortest possible deadline**, and let the attorney decide whether a longer one applies.

## A hard outer limit: the statute of repose

A **statute of repose** is a fixed outer limit that runs from an event (such as the date a product was sold, a building was finished or a medical procedure was done), **no matter when the injury was discovered** and often regardless of tolling. Repose periods are common in medical malpractice, product liability and construction defect claims. A case can be inside the SOL thanks to the discovery rule and still be barred by a statute of repose. If a lead mentions an old surgery, an old product or an old building, note the dates and flag it.

## Example deadlines

These are **examples only**, for general negligence claims (car accidents, slip and falls and the like). Other claim types in the same state can be different. The attorney confirms the deadline on every file, and these laws change, so check the current statute before relying on any number here.

| State | General negligence / personal injury | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Florida | **2 years** for claims arising on or after **March 24, 2023** | Before HB 837 (2023) it was 4 years, so older files may still be on 4 years. Fla. Stat. § 95.11 |
| Texas | 2 years | Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 16.003 |
| California | 2 years | Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 335.1 |
| New York | 3 years | N.Y. CPLR 214 |
| Georgia | 2 years | O.C.G.A. § 9-3-33 |

Notice that the most common period is **two years**, and that it can feel long at intake and very short by the time treatment ends. Many PI cases are still in treatment or negotiation a year or more after the accident. That is why the deadline is calendared on day one.

## Claims against the government: much shorter notice deadlines

When the at-fault party is a **government body or employee** (a city bus, a police car, a public school, a county road crew, a state hospital, a pothole on a public road), special rules apply. Governments have **sovereign immunity**, and each state's law allows claims against it only on its own terms. Those terms usually include a **written notice of claim** that must be filed with the right agency long before the normal SOL, and sometimes a waiting period before suit can be filed.

Examples:

| Where | Notice rule (example) |
|---|---|
| California | A written claim to the public entity, generally within **6 months** of the injury for personal injury claims (Cal. Gov. Code § 911.2) |
| New York | A **notice of claim** generally within **90 days** against a city, county or other public corporation (N.Y. Gen. Mun. Law § 50-e) |
| Florida | **Written notice** to the agency (and, for most claims, to the Department of Financial Services) is required before suit can be filed, and the agency is given time to respond (Fla. Stat. § 768.28(6)). The time limits are set in the statute; the attorney confirms them. |
| Other states | Many have their own notice rules, and some cities and counties set even shorter ones in their charters |

These deadlines can be **months, not years**, and missing one can end the claim. Federal government defendants (a USPS truck, a military vehicle, a VA hospital) fall under the Federal Tort Claims Act, which has its own administrative claim process.

**Red flags to raise the same day:** a government vehicle or employee, a public bus or train, a public school, a public hospital, a government building or sidewalk, a road defect, a police chase, a postal truck.

## Insurance deadlines that are not lawsuits

Insurance policies and insurance laws also set deadlines. These do not end the lawsuit the way an SOL does, but missing them can cost the client benefits.

- **Prompt notice of the claim.** Most policies require the insured to report an accident "promptly" or "as soon as practicable." Late notice can give the insurer a reason to deny. This is one reason the claims team opens claims quickly (Days 10–12).
- **Florida PIP: the 14-day rule.** Under Florida's no-fault law, an injured person generally has to receive **initial medical treatment within 14 days** of the accident to be eligible for Personal Injury Protection (PIP) benefits (Fla. Stat. § 627.736). If a Florida lead was hurt recently and has not seen a doctor yet, this is urgent: tell your lead or the attorney right away.
- **UM/UIM claims.** Uninsured and underinsured motorist claims are made against the client's **own** policy. The policy may require notice within a certain time, consent before settling with the at-fault driver, or suit or arbitration within a contractual period that can differ from the SOL. The attorney reads the policy; you make sure the policy and declarations page are in the file.
- **Health plan and lien notices.** Health plans, Medicare and Medicaid have their own reporting requirements once a claim is made. These are handled by the lien team (Days 17–18), but they start with accurate dates at intake.

## The VA's role

You are not the person who decides the deadline. You are the person who makes sure the attorney **never finds out about it too late**.

### At intake (Days 7–9)

- **Get the exact date of loss** and write it in the firm's format. Ask for the date, not "about two months ago." If the caller does not know, ask what they can find (the police report, a medical record, a text or photo with a date) and note that the date is unconfirmed.
- **Ask where it happened** (city, county and state). The state decides the law.
- **Ask who was involved**, and listen for government vehicles, employees or property.
- **Ask whether the injured person was a minor** at the time, and whether anyone has died.
- **Ask when they first got medical treatment**, especially in a no-fault state like Florida.
- **Note any earlier attorney.** A lead who had a lawyer before may be close to a deadline, and the file may need the old firm's records.

### On every file

- **Enter the DOL and the SOL date** in the case system's fields, exactly as the firm requires.
- **Calendar the deadline and its reminders** (for example 180, 90, 60 and 30 days out, or whatever the firm's policy says) on the attorney's calendar and the case calendar (Day 6).
- **Calendar notice deadlines separately** for any government claim, and for insurance deadlines the attorney gives you.
- **Never change or delete** a deadline without the attorney's instruction, and note who told you to and when.

### Escalate immediately

Tell your team lead and the attorney **the same day**, in writing, if:

- the accident is more than about a year old and no lawsuit has been filed,
- a government body, vehicle or employee is involved,
- the client was a minor, has died or may be incapacitated,
- the dates in the file do not match each other (police report vs. medical records vs. intake),
- the client had an earlier attorney who withdrew,
- you are not sure what the deadline is.

## What you must never do

- **Never tell a lead or client how long they have to file.** "You have two years" is legal advice, and it can be wrong (tolling, government notice rules, a different claim type, a different state). It is also a promise the firm may not be able to keep.
- **Never tell a lead they are "too late"** or that they do not have a case. Only an attorney decides that. Take the information and pass it on.
- **Never guess a deadline** or copy one from another file.
- **Never assume** that opening a claim, sending a demand or being in negotiations stops the clock. It does not.

If a caller asks, a safe answer is:

> "That's a great question for the attorney. Deadlines depend on the details of each case, so I'll make sure the attorney reviews your dates right away and someone gets back to you."

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Writing the date the caller called instead of the accident date | Every deadline is calculated from the wrong day | Confirm the DOL in the caller's words and against the police report |
| Mixing up day and month (03/04 vs. 04/03) | A one-month error on a deadline | Use the firm's date format every time and read the date back to the caller |
| Assuming a child's case is tolled | Some states don't toll, and the parent's claim may not be tolled | Flag every minor and let the attorney decide |
| Not noticing a city bus or police car | Government notice deadlines can be a few months | Ask who owned the vehicle or property; flag it the same day |
| Calendaring only the final deadline | No warning before it's too late | Calendar the reminders too |
| Thinking negotiations protect the case | The SOL keeps running | Keep the deadline visible on the file until suit is filed or the case settles |
| Missing Florida's 14-day PIP treatment rule | The client loses PIP benefits | Ask when they first saw a doctor and flag anything close to 14 days |

## Quick reference

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Date of loss (DOL)** | The day the accident or injury happened |
| **Statute of limitations (SOL)** | The deadline to **file a lawsuit** |
| **Accrual** | The day the SOL clock starts |
| **Discovery rule** | In some claims, the clock starts when the injury was or should have been discovered |
| **Tolling** | A pause in the clock (minors, incapacity, absent defendant), depending on the state |
| **Statute of repose** | A fixed outer limit that runs from an event, regardless of discovery |
| **Notice of claim** | A written claim that must be given to a government body, often within months |
| **Prompt notice** | A policy's requirement to report the claim to the insurer quickly |
| **Florida PIP 14-day rule** | Initial treatment within 14 days of the accident to qualify for PIP |

**The rules for staff:** record the exact DOL, location and parties · calendar the deadline and its reminders · flag government, minor, death, old-accident and prior-attorney cases the same day · never tell a caller how long they have.
