## Documenting economic damages: medical specials, lost wages and out-of-pocket losses

A personal injury claim asks for two kinds of money. **Non-economic damages** are for pain, suffering and loss of enjoyment of life; they have no receipt (see *How to calculate non-economic damages*). **Economic damages** are the losses you can **count**: medical bills, lost pay, money the client spent because of the injury. They are often called **"specials"** (special damages).

Economic damages are only as strong as their paper. A lost-wage claim with no employer letter, or a medical total missing a provider, is money the client doesn't get. This page explains each category, what proves it, how to track it and how it feeds the demand.

## 1. The categories

| Category | What it covers | Past or future? |
|---|---|---|
| **Medical expenses** | Every accident-related provider bill: ER, ambulance, hospital, doctors, imaging, chiropractic, therapy, surgery, prescriptions, equipment | Both |
| **Lost wages** | Pay the client lost from missed work | Past |
| **Loss of earning capacity** | Reduced ability to earn in the future because of the injury | Future |
| **Out-of-pocket expenses** | Mileage to treatment, parking, prescriptions, medical supplies, copays and deductibles | Mostly past |
| **Property damage** | Vehicle repair or total-loss value, personal property in the vehicle, rental car or loss of use | Past |
| **Household services** | Paid help (cleaning, childcare, yard work) the client needed because they couldn't do it | Past and sometimes future |
| **Other** | Funeral and burial costs (wrongful death), home modifications, education or retraining | Varies |

## 2. Medical expenses (medical specials)

### Past medical expenses

What proves them:

- **Itemized bills** from every provider (CMS-1500 for professionals, UB-04 for facilities; see *Reading medical bills and records*).
- **Ledgers** showing payments, adjustments and balances.
- **Records** that show each treatment was for the accident injuries.
- **EOBs** and lien itemizations showing what insurers paid.
- **Pharmacy printouts** for prescriptions.

Track **billed, paid, adjusted and outstanding** amounts for each provider, not just a total. The attorney decides which figure is presented, and the rules differ by state (see section 8).

### Future medical expenses

When the client will need more care (surgery recommended but not yet done, ongoing injections, lifelong therapy), the attorney may claim future medical costs. What proves them:

- A **doctor's written recommendation** or narrative report saying what treatment is needed and that it is because of the accident.
- **Cost estimates** from the provider or a surgery center.
- In larger cases, a **life care plan** from a life care planner, and an economist's report.

Your role: request narrative reports and estimates when the case manager asks, and make sure recommendations in the records are flagged.

## 3. Lost wages

Lost wages are pay the client actually lost because of the injury: missed days, reduced hours, lost overtime, used sick or vacation time (in many states, used PTO counts as a loss).

### What proves them

| Client's situation | Documents |
|---|---|
| **Employee (W-2)** | **Employer wage verification letter** (on company letterhead, signed: job title, rate of pay, usual hours, dates missed, pay lost, PTO used) · pay stubs from before and after the accident · W-2s |
| **Self-employed / contractor (1099)** | **Tax returns** (usually 2–3 years before the accident) · 1099s · profit and loss statements · invoices, contracts and bank statements showing lost jobs or reduced income · a letter from the client's accountant |
| **Gig worker** (rideshare, delivery) | Earnings statements from the app platforms for periods before and after the accident |
| **Any client** | **Doctor's work notes** or **disability slips** saying the client couldn't work, or could work only with restrictions, for specific dates |

**The doctor's note is the link.** Without a medical reason for missing work, an insurer will argue the client chose not to work. Each lost-wage period should match a work note or a record that shows the restriction.

### Calculating it

The attorney or demand drafter does the calculation, but you should know the shape of it:

**Rate of pay × hours (or days) missed = lost wages for the period**

For self-employed clients, it is usually based on average income before the accident compared with income after, which is why several years of tax returns are needed.

### The wage verification request

Use the firm's wage verification form. Send it to the employer's HR or payroll with the client's signed **employment authorization**, and follow up as you would a records request. Note who you spoke to and when it is promised.

## 4. Loss of earning capacity

This is a **future** loss: the injury reduced what the client can earn for the rest of their working life (for example a warehouse worker who can no longer lift and must take a lower-paying job). It is usually supported by:

- Doctor's reports on **permanent restrictions** or impairment ratings.
- **Vocational expert** reports on what work the client can do now.
- **Economist** reports calculating the lost earnings over time.
- The client's work history, education and earnings records.

This is an attorney-level claim. Your role is to gather the employment and earnings history and the medical reports when asked.

## 5. Out-of-pocket expenses

Small amounts add up. Ask the client to keep **every receipt** and a **mileage log**, and remind them at the 2-week calls.

| Expense | Proof |
|---|---|
| **Mileage to and from treatment** | A log with dates, provider and round-trip miles (the attorney decides the per-mile rate; many firms use the IRS medical mileage rate) |
| **Parking, tolls, rideshare to appointments** | Receipts |
| **Prescriptions** | Pharmacy receipts or printout |
| **Over-the-counter medicine, braces, supplies** | Receipts |
| **Copays, deductibles** | Receipts, EOBs, provider ledgers |
| **Medical equipment** (crutches, TENS unit, shower chair) | Receipts or the provider's bill |
| **Paid household help or childcare** | Invoices, receipts, a signed statement from the helper |

## 6. Property damage and rental

The property damage claim is often handled separately from the injury claim, and sometimes earlier (see *What happens after a car accident in Florida? The property damage claim process*). Still, document it in the case:

- **Repair estimate and final repair invoice**, or the **total-loss valuation** and settlement letter.
- **Photos** of the vehicle damage (also useful to show the force of the impact).
- **Rental car** receipts or **loss of use** dates.
- **Personal property** damaged in the accident (phone, car seat, laptop, eyeglasses): receipts or replacement-cost evidence.
- **Diminished value**, where the state allows it and the attorney pursues it.

## 7. Building the damages tracker

Most firms keep a damages tracker in the case system or a spreadsheet. Typical columns:

| Column | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Category** | Medical, lost wages, out-of-pocket, property, other |
| **Provider / source** | Who billed it or where the loss came from |
| **Dates** | Dates of service or period of loss |
| **Billed / claimed amount** | Full amount |
| **Paid by** | Health insurer, PIP, MedPay, Medicare, Medicaid, client |
| **Amount paid** | Per payer |
| **Adjustments / write-offs** | |
| **Outstanding balance** | |
| **Lien?** | Yes/no and lienholder |
| **Document on file** | The file name of the bill, letter or receipt that proves it |
| **Status** | Requested · received · verified · final |
| **Last verified** | Date of the last balance check |

Rules for a good tracker:

- **Every number has a document.** If you can't point to the bill, letter or receipt, it isn't proven.
- **Update it after every balance verification** and before the demand goes out.
- **Mark future damages separately** from past damages.
- **Never round or estimate** a figure; record exactly what the document says.

## 8. Collateral sources and what amount counts

Clients often ask: "If my health insurance paid my bills, can I still claim them?" The answer depends on state law.

- The traditional **collateral source rule** says the at-fault party doesn't get credit for benefits the client received from other sources (health insurance, PIP, disability), so the client can still claim those medical expenses.
- **States differ** on how it works today. Some states reduce the award by collateral payments after the verdict; some limit medical damages to the amounts **actually paid or owed** rather than the full billed amount; some limit what evidence of billed amounts a jury can hear. **Florida, for example, since its 2023 tort reform (HB 837), limits the evidence of past and future medical expenses** in many cases to amounts actually paid, owed or payable under the client's insurance.
- Even where the client can claim the bills, the payer may have a **lien** (see *Liens in personal injury cases*), so the money may have to be repaid.

This is why you track **billed, paid and owed** for every provider: the attorney picks the right figure for the state and the case.

## 9. How economic damages feed the demand

The demand letter usually has a damages section that lists:

1. **Medical specials** by provider, with dates of service and amounts, totaled.
2. **Future medical** costs, with the supporting recommendation.
3. **Lost wages**, with the dates and calculation.
4. **Out-of-pocket expenses**.
5. **Non-economic damages** (pain and suffering), which the attorney values separately.

Economic damages also influence non-economic damages: insurers and evaluation software often look at the medical specials and the type of treatment when valuing pain and suffering. Missing medical bills lower both.

Before the demand goes out, run the **pre-demand check** (see *Pre-Demand Requirements*): every provider's records and itemized bills in, balances verified, wage documents in, the tracker matches the documents.

## 10. Your role and its limits

**You do:** request bills, records, wage letters and tax documents; remind clients to keep receipts and mileage logs; keep the damages tracker accurate and up to date; flag missing documents; prepare the figures for the demand drafter.

**You don't:** decide which amounts to claim, calculate future losses or earning capacity, tell a client what their claim is worth, or tell a client whether health-insurance-paid bills "count." Pass those questions to the attorney.

## Common mistakes

- Missing a provider (the ambulance, the radiologist, the pharmacy).
- Using a **summary bill** or a phone balance instead of an itemized, written bill.
- A lost-wage claim with no **doctor's work note** for the missed dates.
- An employer letter that's **unsigned or not on letterhead**.
- Only one year of tax returns for a self-employed client.
- Not asking the client for **receipts and mileage** until the end of the case.
- Recording only billed amounts, or only balances.
- Mixing **future** and **past** damages in one line.
- A tracker number with no document behind it.

## Quick reference

| Damage | Key proof |
|---|---|
| Past medical | Itemized bills + records + ledgers/EOBs |
| Future medical | Doctor's recommendation or narrative + cost estimate (life care plan in large cases) |
| Lost wages (employee) | Employer wage verification letter + pay stubs + doctor's work notes |
| Lost wages (self-employed) | 2–3 years of tax returns + 1099s/P&L + proof of lost work + work notes |
| Earning capacity | Permanent restrictions + vocational and economic reports |
| Out-of-pocket | Receipts + mileage log |
| Property damage | Estimate/invoice or total-loss letter + photos + rental receipts |
| What figure counts? | Depends on state law; track billed, paid and owed |
