## When the injured person can't sign for themselves

Most intake calls are with the injured person: an adult who can tell their own story, sign their own retainer and make their own decisions. Some are not. The injured person may be a **child**, an **adult who can no longer make decisions**, or someone who **has died**. In each of these cases, the person on the phone is calling on someone else's behalf, and the law has special rules about **who can hire the firm, who owns the claim, who can settle it and what a court has to approve**.

Getting this wrong at intake can mean the wrong person signs the retainer, the firm cannot settle the case without going back to court, or a deadline is missed. These calls are also some of the most emotional ones you will take: a parent whose child was hurt, a spouse whose partner is in a coma, a family that has just lost someone.

This page covers, for each of the three situations:

- who usually signs and who owns the claim,
- what the court may have to approve,
- the documents to request,
- how to handle the call with care,
- the red flags to raise with the attorney.

It builds on the intake call flow, the intake packet and the red flags taught on Days 7–9.

## The general rule

An adult with full capacity signs for themselves. Anyone else signs **only with legal authority** to act for the injured person or their estate. The attorney decides who has that authority; your job is to **find out who the caller is, what their relationship is, and what papers they have**, and to never let the caller assume they can sign just because they are family.

| Injured person | Who usually acts for them | What proves it |
|---|---|---|
| Minor (under 18 in most states) | Parent or legal guardian | Birth certificate; guardianship or custody order if not a parent |
| Incapacitated adult | Court-appointed guardian (or conservator), or an agent under a valid power of attorney, depending on the state and the document | Letters of guardianship; the power of attorney |
| Deceased person | The **personal representative** (executor or administrator) of the estate, or the persons the state's wrongful death law names | Letters of administration / letters testamentary; death certificate |

## Minors

### Two claims, not one

When a child is hurt, there are often **two separate claims**:

1. **The child's claim**: for the child's own pain and suffering, and in some cases future losses. It belongs to the **child**, even though a parent brings it for them.
2. **The parent's claim**: in many states, for the **medical bills** the parents paid or owe for the child's treatment, and sometimes for the parents' own losses. It belongs to the **parents**.

The two claims can have **different deadlines**. In many states the child's own deadline is paused until they turn 18, but the parent's claim for medical expenses may not be. Some states do not pause the child's deadline either, or pause it only in limited cases. (See *Statutes of limitations and other claim deadlines in personal injury*.) Flag every minor's case and let the attorney calendar it.

### Who signs

- Usually a **parent** signs the retainer for the child. If the parents are divorced or separated, ask who has legal custody: the attorney may want both parents involved, or the custodial parent's papers.
- A **grandparent, aunt, older sibling or family friend** usually cannot sign just because the child lives with them. They need a **guardianship** or custody order. Don't refuse the call; take the information and flag it.
- A minor **should not sign** anything themselves.

### Settling a minor's claim

Because a child cannot legally settle their own claim, many states require extra protection:

- **Court approval** of the settlement, often above a certain amount (the threshold and procedure vary by state; Florida, for example, has a statutory threshold above which court approval and a guardianship are required).
- A **guardian ad litem** (an independent person the court appoints to protect the child's interests) in some cases.
- The money is often placed in a **restricted (blocked) account**, a **structured settlement** or a **trust** until the child turns 18, rather than paid to the parents.

This affects timing and costs. The attorney explains it to the family; you make sure the file shows the child's date of birth, the parents' names and the custody situation from the start.

### Documents to request (minor)

- The child's **birth certificate** (proves age and parentage).
- **Custody or guardianship order**, if the caller is not a parent or the parents are separated.
- The child's health insurance card (often the parent's plan, or **Medicaid/CHIP**: Medicaid will usually have a lien, which the lien team handles on Days 17–18).
- School records may later be needed to show missed school or effects on learning.

## Incapacitated adults

An adult may be unable to make decisions because of the accident itself (a traumatic brain injury, a coma) or because of an existing condition (dementia, a serious disability).

### Who can act

- A **court-appointed guardian** (called a conservator in some states) can usually act for the person within the powers the court gave them.
- An **agent under a durable power of attorney (POA)** may be able to hire a lawyer, depending on what the POA says and state law. A POA signed **after** the person lost capacity is generally not valid, and an ordinary POA usually ends when the person dies.
- A **spouse or adult child** does not automatically have authority to sign legal contracts for another adult, even if they are the person's health care surrogate at the hospital.

If no one has authority, the attorney may need to start a **guardianship** in court before the case can go forward. You do not decide this; you capture the facts:

- What is the person's condition right now? Are they able to talk or understand?
- Is there a guardian, a POA or any court papers? Who has them?
- Who else is in the family (spouse, adult children, parents)?

### Documents to request (incapacity)

- **Letters of guardianship** or the court order appointing the guardian or conservator.
- The **power of attorney** document, the whole thing, with its date and signature page.
- Hospital information, so the records team can identify the provider (the HIPAA authorization will need to be signed by the right person; see the sample onboarding packet).

## Deceased persons: wrongful death and survival claims

When someone dies because of another person's negligence, the law usually allows **two kinds of claims**. States combine or name them differently, but the idea is common:

| | **Wrongful death claim** | **Survival claim (survival action)** |
|---|---|---|
| Whose loss | The **survivors'** losses from the death | The **deceased person's own** losses before death |
| Typical damages | Loss of support and services, loss of companionship and guidance, funeral expenses (depending on the state) | The person's medical bills, lost wages and, in some states, their pain and suffering between the injury and death |
| Who brings it | Depends on the state: in some states the **personal representative** of the estate brings it for all survivors (Florida, for example); in others certain **heirs or family members** bring it directly (California, for example) | Usually the **personal representative** of the estate |

### The estate usually has to be opened

In most cases the firm will need someone with **legal authority over the estate**: the **personal representative** (called an executor if named in a will, or an administrator if there was no will). That authority comes from the **probate court**, as **letters of administration** or **letters testamentary**. Being next of kin, or being named in the will, is not the same as having been appointed.

So a typical wrongful death intake involves:

- finding out whether an estate has been opened, and who was appointed,
- if not, who would be the right person to serve (the attorney decides; often the surviving spouse, an adult child or a parent),
- identifying **all** the survivors who may have a claim (spouse, children, parents, and in some states others), because the attorney may need to notify or include them.

Wrongful death claims have **their own deadlines**, which often start from the **date of death**, not the date of the accident. Flag them the same day.

### Documents to request (death)

- The **death certificate** (certified copy).
- **Letters of administration** or **letters testamentary**, if an estate is open, and the probate case number.
- The **will**, if there is one.
- A list of **survivors**: spouse, children (including adult children and children from other relationships), parents, with ages and contact details.
- **Funeral and burial bills**, and who paid them.
- The **autopsy report** and medical examiner's report, if any, and the hospital records.
- Information on the deceased's **employment and income**, for loss of support.

## How to handle the call

These callers are frightened, grieving or exhausted. How the firm sounds on the first call is how they will remember it.

- **Slow down.** Let them talk. Don't rush to the checklist.
- **Express care simply and sincerely:** "I'm so sorry for your loss." "I'm sorry this happened to your son." Don't overdo it, and don't use phrases like "I know how you feel."
- **Use the injured person's name** once you have it, and the right tense: don't talk about a person who has died in the present tense.
- **Explain why you are asking** about documents: "The attorney will need to know who has legal authority to act for your mother, so I'm going to ask a few questions about that."
- **Don't promise anything:** not that they can sign, not that the case is strong, not how long it will take.
- **Never ask about money first.** Ask about what happened and who was hurt; the attorney discusses damages.
- **Offer a follow-up**: "The attorney will review this today and someone will call you back. Is this the best number?"

## Red flags to raise with the attorney the same day

- The caller is not a parent, guardian or appointed representative, but wants to sign.
- **Parents disagree**, are divorced or one cannot be found.
- Several family members are calling separately about the same death, or they **disagree** about who should be in charge.
- Another lawyer may already be representing the family or the estate.
- The injured adult **seems able to speak for themselves** but a relative insists on handling everything (the attorney needs to speak to the client).
- The person died **some time after** the accident, or died of something that may or may not be connected.
- A government vehicle, employee or property is involved (short notice deadlines).
- Signs of **abuse or neglect** (for example, a child or vulnerable adult hurt in someone's care). Tell your lead immediately; the attorney decides what must be reported.
- A minor's or decedent's case is old, or the date of death or accident is unclear.

## What you must never do

- **Never tell a caller they can or cannot bring the claim**, or who "should" be the personal representative. That is legal advice.
- **Never let someone without authority sign** the retainer or HIPAA authorization just to move things along.
- **Never tell a family how long they have**, or that a child's case "can wait until they're 18."
- **Never discuss the value** of a death or a child's injury.
- **Never share one family member's information with another** without the attorney's direction. Family members may have different interests.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Letting a grandparent sign for a grandchild without a guardianship | The retainer may not be valid | Ask for custody or guardianship papers; flag it |
| Treating the child's and parent's claims as one | Different deadlines; one can be missed | Record the child's DOB and both parents' details; flag the minor |
| Assuming the spouse can act for a hospitalized adult | A spouse isn't automatically a legal guardian or agent | Ask about a POA or guardianship |
| Using the accident date as the only date in a death case | Wrongful death deadlines may run from the date of death | Record both dates |
| Not asking whether an estate is open | The attorney can't file or settle without a personal representative | Ask about probate and letters |
| Missing survivors (adult children, children from another relationship) | Their rights may be affected | Ask for the full list of family |
| Rushing a grieving caller through the script | Damages trust and leaves gaps in the facts | Slow down; explain why you ask |

## Quick reference

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Minor** | A person under the age of majority (18 in most states) |
| **Guardian** | A person a court appointed to act for a minor or incapacitated adult |
| **Guardian ad litem** | An independent person the court appoints to protect a minor's interests in a case |
| **Conservator** | In some states, the court-appointed manager of an incapacitated person's finances |
| **Power of attorney (POA)** | A document letting an agent act for someone; usually ends at death |
| **Personal representative** | The executor or administrator of a deceased person's estate |
| **Letters of administration / testamentary** | The probate court's document appointing the personal representative |
| **Wrongful death claim** | The survivors' claim for their losses from the death |
| **Survival claim** | The estate's claim for the deceased's own losses before death |
| **Restricted (blocked) account** | An account a minor's settlement is held in until they come of age |

**The rules for staff:** find out who the caller is and what authority they have · request the papers that prove it · record every date (DOB, date of accident, date of death) · flag every minor, incapacity and death case the same day · be kind, slow and careful on the call · never decide who can sign.
