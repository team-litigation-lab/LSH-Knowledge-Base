## Why ethics rules apply to you

Lawyers are licensed by a state bar and must follow that state's **Rules of Professional Conduct**. Almost every state bases its rules on the **American Bar Association (ABA) Model Rules of Professional Conduct**, so the rule numbers in this article (Rule 1.6, Rule 5.3 and so on) are the same, or very close, in most states.

You are not a lawyer, so the bar cannot discipline you. But that does not mean the rules don't reach you. Under **Rule 5.3**, the lawyer you work for is responsible for making sure that what you do is compatible with the lawyer's own duties. If a VA breaks a rule, the attorney can be disciplined for it, the client can be harmed, and the firm can lose the case or its reputation. In practice, a firm will hold you to the same standard it holds its lawyers.

This article is a plain-English tour of the rules that come up most in the work you learn in the Foundational Training: reception (Days 3–5), calendar management (Day 6), intake (Days 7–9), insurance communication (Days 10–12), provider communication (Days 13–16) and lien work (Days 17–18).

Two topics have their own articles in the library, so they are only summarized here:

- **Unauthorized Practice of Law (UPL):** what counts as legal advice and why only an attorney can give it.
- **Understanding Attorney-Client Privilege and Confidentiality Practices:** what privilege protects and how to keep it.

## The rules at a glance

| Rule | What it says, in short | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|
| **5.3** Responsibilities regarding nonlawyer assistance | Lawyers must supervise non-lawyer staff and are responsible for their conduct. | Every task you do |
| **1.6** Confidentiality of information | Everything about a client's matter is confidential unless the client agrees or an exception applies. | Calls, emails, screens, files |
| **1.4** Communication | The client must be kept reasonably informed and their questions answered promptly. | Client calls, 2-week calls, status updates |
| **4.2** Communication with a represented person | You may not contact someone about the matter if you know a lawyer represents them, unless that lawyer agrees. | Calls to the other side, to defendants |
| **4.3** Dealing with an unrepresented person | Don't suggest you are neutral; don't give legal advice to someone without a lawyer. | Witnesses, the at-fault driver, lead callers |
| **7.3** Solicitation of clients | No live, pressured solicitation of people who haven't asked; no paying "runners" or "cappers". | Intake, reception, marketing |
| **7.2** Advertising / paying for referrals | Generally no paying anyone for recommending the firm, with narrow exceptions. | Referrals, lead sources |
| **1.15** Safekeeping property | Client money goes in a trust account (often IOLTA), kept separate and fully recorded. | Settlement checks, costs, disbursements |
| **1.8(e)** Financial assistance | A lawyer may not give a client money for living expenses during a case (with narrow exceptions). | Clients asking for a loan or advance |
| **1.7** Conflicts of interest | A lawyer can't take a matter that conflicts with another client's. | Intake conflict checks |
| **5.5** Unauthorized practice of law | Only licensed lawyers practice law. | Any question that asks "what should I do?" |

## Rule 5.3: the attorney supervises you

Rule 5.3 says a lawyer who manages or directly supervises non-lawyers must make **reasonable efforts** to ensure their conduct is compatible with the lawyer's professional obligations. That includes staff who work remotely and outside contractors, which is what a VA usually is.

What this means for you:

- **You work under an attorney's direction.** Your SOPs, scripts and checklists are how that supervision happens. Follow them.
- **When something is not covered, ask.** "I'm not sure, let me check with the attorney" is always an acceptable answer. Guessing is not.
- **Anything legal goes to the lawyer.** Signing documents as the attorney, deciding whether to accept an offer, deciding to file suit, or telling a client what their case is worth are lawyer decisions.
- **Mistakes must be reported, not hidden.** A late deadline, a misdirected email or a wrong document sent can often be fixed if the attorney knows quickly. Hiding it is what turns it into a disciplinary problem.

## Rule 1.6: confidentiality

Rule 1.6 is broader than attorney-client privilege. It covers **all information relating to the representation**, whatever the source: the client's name, the fact that they are a client, their injuries, treatment, insurance, finances, family and everything in the file. Rule 1.6(c) also requires the firm to make reasonable efforts to prevent unauthorized access to that information, which is why information security matters (see *Information security for remote legal VAs*).

Practical rules:

- **Confirm who you are speaking with** before discussing a case. Use the firm's verification steps (for example full name, date of birth and date of loss) for any caller who claims to be the client.
- **A family member is not the client.** A spouse, parent or adult child calling about the case should not get case details unless the client has authorized it in writing and it is noted in the file. Take a message instead.
- **Don't confirm someone is a client** to an outsider who calls asking ("Is John Smith a client of yours?"). Take their name and number and pass it on.
- **Share only what the task needs.** A provider getting a records request needs the patient's identity and the authorization, not the accident story. A 3P adjuster opening a claim needs the name and plate, not the client's DOB (see *Opening a 3P claim: what you can and can't share with the insurer*).
- **No case talk in public**, on social media or with friends, even without names. Details are often enough to identify someone.

## Rule 1.4: keeping the client informed

The most common complaint to state bars is not that a lawyer lost a case. It is that **the lawyer never called back**. Rule 1.4 requires the lawyer to keep the client reasonably informed about the status of the matter and to respond promptly to reasonable requests for information. The lawyer must also tell the client about any settlement offer, because only the client can decide whether to accept it.

The support team carries much of this duty day to day:

- **Return calls and messages the same business day** when you can, or follow the firm's response-time standard.
- **Make the 2-week calls** and status calls on schedule and note them (Days 13–16 cover the 2-week call).
- **Never sit on a message for the attorney.** If a client wants to speak to the lawyer, log it and route it, and tell the client when to expect a call.
- **Pass on every offer.** If an adjuster tells you about an offer, write down the exact figure and terms and give it to the attorney immediately. Do not tell the client the offer is good or bad.
- **Note every contact.** If it isn't in the case notes, the firm cannot prove the client was informed.

## Rule 4.2: don't contact a represented person

Under Rule 4.2, a lawyer, and staff acting for the lawyer, may not communicate about the subject of the matter with a person **known to be represented by a lawyer** in that matter, unless that lawyer consents or the law allows it.

How this works in a personal injury firm:

- **The other driver (the defendant or tortfeasor):** once you know they have a lawyer, all contact goes through that lawyer. Before a lawyer is involved, communication with the at-fault party usually goes through their insurer.
- **Insurance adjusters** are not lawyers representing a party, so calling them is normal claims work. But if the insurer has hired **defense counsel** (common once a lawsuit is filed), the attorney will tell you whether calls go to counsel instead.
- **The reverse protects our clients.** Once the firm sends a Letter of Representation (LOR), the other side should contact the firm, not the client. If a client says an adjuster or defense lawyer called them directly, note it and tell the attorney right away.

## Rule 4.3: people without a lawyer

When you speak with someone who has **no lawyer**, such as a witness, an unrepresented at-fault driver or a lead caller who has not signed up, Rule 4.3 says you must not suggest you are neutral or disinterested, and you must not give them legal advice (other than advice to get a lawyer) if their interests may conflict with the client's.

- Say who you are and who you work for: "I'm calling from [Firm], we represent [client] in connection with the accident on [date]."
- Don't tell a witness or an at-fault driver what they should say, sign or do.
- Lead callers who have not signed get information about the firm and the next step (a consultation), not an opinion on their case.

## Rule 7.3 and 7.2: how clients may be found

These rules control how a firm gets clients. They matter most at **reception and intake**.

- **No pressured solicitation.** Rule 7.3 prohibits live person-to-person contact (in person, by live phone call or real-time chat) to solicit someone who has not asked for help, when the purpose is the lawyer's financial gain. There are narrow exceptions (for example a former client or a family member). Calling accident victims from a police report list to sign them up is the classic example of what is not allowed. Many states add their own rules, such as waiting periods after an accident before written solicitation may be sent.
- **No runners or cappers.** A "runner" or "capper" is someone paid to steer accident victims to a lawyer, for example a tow-truck driver, hospital worker or chiropractor paid per referral. Paying for referrals is generally prohibited by Rule 7.2 and is a crime in many states. If someone offers to send the firm cases "for a fee", don't agree to anything; pass it to the attorney.
- **Inbound callers are fine.** When a person calls the firm because they want help, that is not solicitation. Be helpful and professional.
- **Don't pressure, don't promise.** Never tell a lead "you have a great case", "you'll get a big settlement" or "you have to sign today". Statements like these can be misleading under Rule 7.1 and they are also legal opinions.
- **Follow-up must be respectful.** Follow-up calls and texts to people who contacted the firm should follow the firm's script and stop when the person says they are not interested.

## Rule 1.15: client money and trust accounts

Rule 1.15 requires lawyers to keep client and third-party money **separate from the firm's own money**, in a trust account. Small or short-term client funds usually go into an **IOLTA** account (Interest on Lawyers' Trust Accounts), where the interest goes to the state bar foundation rather than to the lawyer or client.

In a personal injury case, a settlement check is made out to the client and the firm. It is deposited into trust, and from there the firm pays medical providers and lien holders, reimburses case costs, takes its fee and pays the client their share, all as shown on a **closing statement** the client signs.

What the support team must know:

- **Never deposit a settlement check into an operating account**, and never hold client money personally.
- **Log every check the day it arrives** (date received, payer, amount, check number) and route it under the firm's procedure.
- **Never promise a client a payment date or amount.** The attorney releases funds once the check clears and liens are resolved.
- **Disputed money stays in trust.** If a provider and the client disagree about a bill, that amount is held until it is resolved.
- **Accurate records matter.** Bars audit trust accounts. A missing entry can be treated as misconduct even when no money is lost.

## Rule 1.8(e): clients asking for money

Personal injury clients are often out of work and under financial pressure. Rule 1.8(e) generally does **not** allow a lawyer to give or lend a client money for living expenses (rent, car payments, groceries) while the case is pending. The lawyer **may** advance court costs and litigation expenses, usually repaid from the recovery. The 2020 amendment to the Model Rule allows a lawyer representing an indigent client pro bono to give modest humanitarian gifts, and some states have their own versions, so the exact rule varies by state.

If a client asks for an advance or a loan:

- Don't say yes or no on the firm's behalf. Say: "I'll let the attorney know you asked, and someone will get back to you."
- Don't recommend a pre-settlement funding company. Whether that is in the client's interest is a legal question for the attorney.
- Note the request in the file.

## Rule 1.7: conflicts of interest (a pointer)

A lawyer cannot represent a client whose interests are directly adverse to another current client, or where the representation would be materially limited by duties to someone else, without informed consent (and some conflicts can't be waived). That is why intake runs a **conflict check** on every party: the potential client, the at-fault party, passengers, owners of the vehicles and businesses involved. A common PI example is a driver and a passenger in the same car: the passenger may have a claim against the driver. See *Navigating Conflicts of Interest in Personal Injury Litigation* for the full topic.

## Rule 5.5: the practice of law (a pointer)

Only a licensed attorney can give legal advice, set fees, accept a case, appear in court or negotiate on a client's behalf without supervision. You can give facts ("the attorney will call you Thursday"), explain the firm's process ("after treatment ends we request the records and bills"), and pass on the attorney's instructions word for word. You cannot tell someone what their rights are, what their case is worth or what they should do. See *Unauthorized Practice of Law*.

## Scenarios

**Reception.** A caller says she is the client's mother and wants to know when her son's settlement check is coming. *What to do:* check the file for a written authorization. If there isn't one, don't confirm details. "I'm not able to discuss a client's case without their authorization, but I'm happy to take a message and have the case team reach out to him."

**Intake.** A lead asks, "Do I have a case? How much do you think I'll get?" *What to do:* collect the information on the intake form and say, "The attorney reviews every case and will talk to you about that. What I can do is get your consultation scheduled." Never estimate a value.

**Intake.** A body shop owner offers to send the firm his customers who were in accidents in exchange for $200 per signed case. *What to do:* don't agree, note the call and tell the attorney. Paying for referrals is generally prohibited.

**Claims.** While checking a claim, you learn the at-fault driver has hired a lawyer. The client wants you to call the driver to ask about their insurance. *What to do:* don't call the driver. Tell the attorney; contact now goes through the driver's lawyer or the insurer.

**Claims.** An adjuster tells you, "Tell your client we'll pay $8,000 if she settles this week." *What to do:* write down the amount, conditions and deadline, put it in the case notes and give it to the attorney immediately. Don't comment on the offer to the client or the adjuster.

**Provider communication.** A provider's billing office asks for a copy of the police report and the client's statement "to update their file". *What to do:* send only what the firm's procedure says goes to providers (usually the authorization and the request). Ask the attorney before releasing anything else.

**Case management.** A client calls, upset that the case has taken a year, and says he's going to complain to the bar. *What to do:* stay calm, listen, note what he says, give him the next concrete step if you know it, and route it to the attorney as urgent. Rule 1.4 problems are fixed with communication.

## Common mistakes

- **Giving case details to a relative or friend** of the client without written authorization.
- **Sitting on a message** for days because the attorney is busy, instead of logging and escalating it.
- **Telling a lead "you have a strong case"** or guessing at a settlement amount.
- **Calling a party who has a lawyer**, or forgetting to tell the attorney that the other side contacted our client directly.
- **Not passing on an offer** right away, or describing it to the client as good or bad.
- **Mishandling a check:** not logging it, leaving it in a drawer, or telling the client when they'll be paid.
- **Promising a client an advance** or recommending a funding company.
- **Fixing a mistake quietly** instead of reporting it.

## Quick reference

| Situation | Do | Don't |
|---|---|---|
| Caller says they are the client | Verify identity with the firm's steps | Discuss the case before verifying |
| Family member asks about the case | Check for written authorization; take a message | Share details without authorization |
| Lead asks what the case is worth | Schedule the consultation | Estimate a value or promise a result |
| Someone offers referrals for a fee | Note it and tell the attorney | Agree or negotiate |
| The other party has a lawyer | Route contact through their lawyer | Call or message the party directly |
| Adjuster makes an offer | Record it exactly and tell the attorney now | Comment on it to anyone |
| Settlement check arrives | Log it and follow the trust procedure | Hold it, deposit it yourself, promise a date |
| Client asks for a loan or advance | Note it, route to the attorney | Say yes or no for the firm |
| You made a mistake | Report it to the attorney right away | Hide it or fix it quietly |
