## Reading medical bills and records: claim forms, codes and balances

In Days 13–16 (Provider Communication Training) you request bills and records and verify balances with providers. Those bills arrive in a few standard formats, full of codes and columns. If you can read them, you can tell at a glance whether a bill is complete, whether the balance makes sense and what the provider actually did for the client.

This page explains the two standard claim forms, the difference between an itemized statement and a ledger, the code sets (CPT, HCPCS, ICD-10-CM and revenue codes), the money columns, EOBs and the red flags to catch before a bill goes into the demand.

## 1. The documents you will see

| Document | What it is | When you need it |
|---|---|---|
| **CMS-1500** (still often called the **HCFA** or "HCFA 1500") | The standard claim form for **professional** services: doctors, chiropractors, physical therapists, orthopedists, pain management, ambulance services, outpatient imaging billed by the professional | The most common bill from treating providers |
| **UB-04** (also called the **CMS-1450**) | The standard claim form for **facility** (institutional) services: hospitals, emergency departments, surgery centers, skilled nursing, hospital outpatient departments | ER visits, hospital stays, surgery at a facility |
| **Itemized statement** | A line-by-line list of every service, with dates, codes and charges | Insurers and lienholders need it; the demand relies on it |
| **Summary bill / statement of account** | Just a total, or a total per visit | Not enough on its own: ask for the itemized version |
| **Ledger** (account history) | Every charge, payment, adjustment and write-off on the account over time, with a running balance | Balance verification; shows who paid what |
| **EOB** (Explanation of Benefits) | The insurer's (or Medicare's) statement of what it was billed, what it allowed, paid and what the patient owes | Proves payments and is the basis of many liens |
| **Medical records** | Notes, reports, imaging results, test results | Prove the injury and the treatment; billing records alone don't |

**Bills are not records, and records are not bills.** A records request and a bills request are often separate, sometimes to different departments. Check that you have both for every provider.

A hospital visit often produces **more than one bill**: the hospital's UB-04 for the facility, plus separate CMS-1500 bills from the emergency physician group, the radiologist who read the scans and sometimes an anesthesiologist. When you see a hospital in the case, ask whether outside physician groups billed too.

## 2. Reading a CMS-1500 (professional claim)

The form has numbered boxes. The ones that matter most to you:

| Box | What it shows | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 2, 3, 5 | Patient's name, date of birth, address | Confirm it's your client |
| 1a, 11 | Insured's ID and the insurance plan | Tells you which health insurer or PIP carrier was billed (possible lien) |
| 10 | Whether the condition relates to employment, an **auto accident** (with state) or another accident | Should match the case; "employment" may mean workers' compensation |
| 14 | Date of current illness or injury | Should match the date of the accident |
| 21 | **Diagnosis codes** (ICD-10-CM), lettered A–L | What the provider says is wrong |
| 24A | **Dates of service** (from–to) | When each service was done |
| 24B | **Place of service** code (for example 11 = office, 23 = emergency room–hospital) | Where it happened |
| 24D | **CPT/HCPCS code** and up to four **modifiers** | What was done |
| 24E | Diagnosis pointer (which letters from box 21 support the line) | Ties the service to an injury |
| 24F | **Charges** for the line | The billed amount |
| 24G | **Units** (days or units) | How many times or 15-minute units |
| 28, 29 | Total charge and amount paid | Quick check against the ledger |
| 33 | Billing provider's name, address and NPI | Who you request a balance from |

## 3. Reading a UB-04 (facility claim)

The UB-04 has numbered "form locators" (FL). The key ones:

| FL | What it shows |
|---|---|
| 4 | **Type of bill** (for example, inpatient vs outpatient) |
| 6 | Statement covers period (from–through) |
| 42 | **Revenue codes**: the department or type of service |
| 43 | Description of each revenue code line |
| 44 | HCPCS/CPT code and rates (mainly for outpatient lines) |
| 45 | Service date |
| 46 | Units of service |
| 47 | **Total charges** per line |
| 50 | Payer(s) billed |
| 67 | Principal and other **diagnosis codes** |

### Revenue codes

Revenue codes are four-digit codes that group facility charges by department. A few you'll see often:

| Revenue code | Department |
|---|---|
| 0250 | Pharmacy |
| 0300 | Laboratory |
| 0320 | Diagnostic radiology (X-ray) |
| 0350 | CT scan |
| 0450 | Emergency room |
| 0610 | MRI |

A UB-04 shows revenue code lines, but it may not list every individual item. If an insurer or lienholder needs more detail, request the **itemized statement** (sometimes called the detailed itemized bill).

## 4. The code sets

### CPT and HCPCS: what was done

**CPT** (Current Procedural Terminology, published by the American Medical Association) codes are five-digit codes for procedures and services. **HCPCS Level II** codes (a letter plus four digits, for example E0730 for a TENS unit, or A-codes for ambulance and supplies) cover supplies, equipment, drugs and services CPT doesn't.

Examples common in personal injury cases:

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 99203 | New patient office visit (low level of medical decision-making) |
| 99213 | Established patient office visit (low level of medical decision-making) |
| 99283 | Emergency department visit (moderate level of medical decision-making) |
| 98941 | Chiropractic manipulative treatment, spinal, 3–4 regions |
| 97110 | Therapeutic exercise, each 15 minutes |
| 97140 | Manual therapy techniques, each 15 minutes |
| 72040 | X-ray of the cervical spine, 2 or 3 views |
| 72141 | MRI of the cervical spine without contrast |
| 72148 | MRI of the lumbar spine without contrast |
| 70450 | CT of the head or brain without contrast |

You don't need to memorize codes. You do need to recognize that **a code describes a service**, that "each 15 minutes" codes are billed in **units**, and that the same code on the same date twice may be a duplicate (or may be two legitimate units; check the units column).

### Modifiers

Modifiers are two characters added to a CPT/HCPCS code to explain something about the service. Examples: **25** (a separate evaluation on the same day as a procedure), **59** (a distinct procedural service), **GP** (services under a physical therapy plan of care), **LT/RT** (left/right side). They affect payment, so they are part of the line; copy them exactly when you record a bill.

### ICD-10-CM: why it was done

**ICD-10-CM** diagnosis codes describe the condition. They are letters and numbers, three to seven characters, for example:

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| S13.4XXA | Sprain of ligaments of the cervical spine, **initial encounter** |
| S33.5XXA | Sprain of ligaments of the lumbar spine, initial encounter |
| M54.2 | Cervicalgia (neck pain) |
| V43.52XA | Car driver injured in collision with another car in a traffic accident, initial encounter (an external cause code) |

For injury codes, the **seventh character** tells you the encounter: **A** = initial (active treatment), **D** = subsequent (healing or recovery phase), **S** = sequela (a late effect). The "X" characters are placeholders.

**Why it matters:** the diagnoses on the bills should match the injuries in the case. A diagnosis that has nothing to do with the accident (for example a routine physical or an unrelated illness) is a sign the bill may include **unrelated treatment**, which the attorney may need to remove from the claim.

## 5. The money columns

This is where most balance mistakes happen. Know what each word means:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Billed / gross charges** | What the provider charged, at its full rates |
| **Allowed amount** | What the insurer agreed is the price under its contract or fee schedule |
| **Paid** | What a payer (health insurer, PIP, MedPay, Medicare, Medicaid) actually paid |
| **Contractual adjustment** | The amount the provider took off because of its contract with the insurer (billed minus allowed) |
| **Write-off** | An amount the provider wrote off and won't collect (contractual, courtesy, bad debt) |
| **Patient responsibility** | Deductible, copay and coinsurance the patient owes |
| **Patient payment** | What the client paid out of pocket |
| **Balance / outstanding** | What is still owed to the provider after payments and adjustments |

A simple check for every provider:

**Billed − (insurance payments + PIP/MedPay payments + patient payments) − adjustments and write-offs = balance**

If the numbers don't add up, the bill or ledger is incomplete. Ask the provider for an updated ledger and note the discrepancy.

### Why both billed and paid matter

- The **billed amount** has traditionally been the "medical specials" figure in many demands.
- The **paid amounts** show who has a potential **lien** or reimbursement right (the health insurer, Medicare, Medicaid; see *Liens in personal injury cases*).
- Some states limit the medical damages evidence to amounts paid or owed rather than billed (Florida, for example, since its 2023 tort reform). The attorney decides what figure goes in the demand; your job is to record all of them accurately.

## 6. EOBs

An **Explanation of Benefits** comes from the payer, not the provider. It lists, for each claim: the provider, date of service, codes, billed amount, allowed amount, what the plan paid, adjustments and patient responsibility, plus remark codes explaining any denial.

Use EOBs to:

- **Confirm payments** shown on a provider's ledger.
- **Build a lien itemization** (which charges the health plan paid and claims back).
- **Spot unrelated charges** when a lienholder's list includes treatment that wasn't for the accident (the attorney may dispute those).

Medicare's version is the **Medicare Summary Notice (MSN)**; Medicare's lien itemization is the **conditional payment letter**.

## 7. Red flags before a bill goes in the file

| Red flag | What it might mean | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Duplicate lines** (same code, same date, same units) | A double charge | Ask the provider to confirm |
| **Missing dates of service** or a summary with no detail | Incomplete bill | Request the itemized statement |
| **Dates before the accident** | Prior treatment, possibly unrelated | Flag to the case manager |
| **Dates after treatment ended** or a provider the client never mentioned | Possible error or another patient's charges | Confirm with the client and provider |
| **Diagnoses unrelated to the accident** | Unrelated treatment mixed in | Flag to the case manager |
| **Balance doesn't match** the ledger, your log or the provider's verbal figure | Missing payments or adjustments | Request an updated ledger in writing |
| **Wrong patient name or DOB** | Another patient's records | Stop; report it (see *HIPAA for legal VAs*) |
| **"Auto accident" box unchecked** or "employment" checked | Billed to the wrong payer, or a workers' comp case | Flag to the case manager |
| **Bill sent to health insurance when there's an LOP** | Provider may later claim both | Note it; the case manager handles it |

## 8. How this feeds the case

- **Treatment tracking:** dates of service show the treatment timeline and any **gaps in treatment** the attorney must explain.
- **Balance verification (Day 16):** you confirm each provider's current balance in writing and record billed, paid, adjusted and outstanding.
- **Medical summary and demand:** the demand drafter uses the itemized bills for the **medical specials** and the records for the injury story. A missing bill means missing damages.
- **Liens:** paid amounts on ledgers and EOBs point to who must be repaid out of the settlement.
- **Disbursement:** before settlement money goes out, every provider balance must be final and in writing.

## 9. Your role and its limits

**You do:** request complete itemized bills and records, read and log them accurately, check that balances add up, flag red flags and keep everything in the case file.

**You don't:** decide whether a charge is related to the accident, whether a code is correct or whether a bill is reasonable, negotiate a balance without instruction, or tell a client what their bills "should" be. You report what you see; the case manager and attorney decide.

## Common mistakes

- Accepting a **summary bill** instead of an itemized statement.
- Recording only the **balance** and not the billed and paid amounts.
- Missing the **separate physician bills** for an ER visit (ER doctor, radiologist).
- Logging "each 15 minutes" codes without the **units**.
- Not noticing **dates of service before the accident** or unrelated diagnoses.
- Taking a balance **over the phone** and not getting it in writing.
- Confusing an **EOB** (from the insurer) with a bill (from the provider).
- Treating a $0 balance as "nothing to note" when a health insurer paid the bill (that payment may be a lien).

## Quick reference

| Question | Where to look |
|---|---|
| Professional or facility bill? | CMS-1500 (HCFA) = professional · UB-04 = facility |
| What was done? | CPT/HCPCS code (CMS-1500 box 24D; UB-04 FL 44) |
| Why was it done? | ICD-10-CM diagnosis (CMS-1500 box 21; UB-04 FL 67) |
| Which department? | Revenue code (UB-04 FL 42) |
| When? | Dates of service (box 24A; FL 45) |
| How much? | Charges (box 24F; FL 47) and units (box 24G; FL 46) |
| Who paid what? | Ledger and EOBs |
| Does the balance make sense? | Billed − payments − adjustments = balance |
| Initial, subsequent or late effect? | 7th character of an injury code: A, D, S |
