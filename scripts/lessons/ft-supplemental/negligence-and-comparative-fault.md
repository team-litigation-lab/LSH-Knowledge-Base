## The legal idea behind almost every PI case

Most personal injury cases a firm takes (car accidents, slip and falls, dog bites, trucking crashes) are **negligence** claims. To win money for the client, the attorney has to show that someone else was careless, that the carelessness caused the injury, and that the injury caused real losses. Then the law of the state decides how much the client's **own** share of the fault reduces what they can recover, or whether it wipes the claim out entirely.

You will never argue negligence in court. But every intake question you ask on Days 7–9, and every police report and adjuster's letter you handle on Days 10–12, is collecting the facts the attorney uses to prove it. Knowing what those facts are for makes you much better at gathering them.

This page covers:

- the four elements of negligence,
- negligence per se and vicarious liability,
- the four fault systems the US states use, with worked examples,
- joint and several liability, briefly,
- what intake asks and why, and what you must never say.

## The four elements of negligence

To prove negligence, the client (the **plaintiff**) usually has to show all four of these. If one is missing, the claim fails.

| Element | The question | Car accident example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Duty** | Did the other person owe the client a duty to be careful? | Every driver owes others on the road a duty to drive with reasonable care. |
| **2. Breach** | Did they fail to meet that duty? | The other driver ran a red light while texting. |
| **3. Causation** | Did that failure cause the injury? | Running the light caused the crash, and the crash caused the client's neck injury. |
| **4. Damages** | Did the client suffer real, provable losses? | Medical bills, lost wages, pain and suffering, a damaged car. |

### Duty

The general duty is **reasonable care**: what a reasonably careful person would do in the same situation. Some relationships carry special duties. A store owes its customers a duty to keep the premises reasonably safe (see the library's page on duty of care and visitor classifications); a doctor owes a patient the standard of care of the profession; a common carrier (a bus or a rideshare) may owe its passengers a higher duty.

### Breach

Breach is the careless act or failure to act: speeding, following too closely, failing to yield, leaving a spill on the floor, not fixing a broken step, not restraining a dog. Evidence of breach includes the police report, citations, photos, video, witness statements and the defendant's own admissions.

### Causation

Causation has two parts:

- **Actual cause ("but for")**: but for the defendant's act, would the injury have happened? If the client would have been hurt anyway, there is no actual cause.
- **Proximate cause**: was the injury a **foreseeable** result of the act? The law does not hold people responsible for every remote consequence, only for the kinds of harm their carelessness could be expected to cause.

Causation is where insurers push hardest on the **medical** side: they argue that the injury was pre-existing, came from a different event, or was not caused by such a "minor" impact. That is why gaps in treatment, prior injuries and later accidents matter so much (see the library's pages on treatment gaps).

### Damages

Without provable harm there is no claim, even if the other side was clearly careless. Damages are **economic** (medical bills, lost wages, property damage, out-of-pocket costs) and **non-economic** (pain and suffering, loss of enjoyment of life; see *How to calculate non-economic damages*). Medical records and bills, which the Records team gathers on Days 13–16, are the proof.

## Negligence per se

In many states, if the defendant broke a **safety law** (a traffic law, a building code) and the law was meant to prevent exactly the kind of harm the client suffered, that violation can establish duty and breach automatically, or create a strong presumption of them. This is **negligence per se**. A traffic citation is therefore useful evidence, although a citation alone does not decide fault, and the rules on whether it can be used at trial vary by state.

## Vicarious liability: when someone else is responsible too

Sometimes a person or company is legally responsible for someone else's negligence. This matters because it can bring in **more insurance**.

- **Respondeat superior (employer liability):** an employer is generally responsible for the negligence of an employee acting **within the scope of their job**. A delivery driver on a route, a company truck on a job: the employer and the employer's commercial policy may be involved.
- **Owner liability:** some states make a vehicle's **owner** responsible when they let someone else drive it. Florida, for example, has the "dangerous instrumentality" doctrine. Others make owners liable only for their own negligence (such as lending a car to someone they knew was unsafe: **negligent entrustment**).
- **Parents** can sometimes be responsible for a minor's driving (for example, where they signed for the minor's license), depending on the state.
- **Negligent hiring, supervision or retention** can make a company responsible for its own carelessness in hiring or keeping a dangerous employee.

This is why intake and claims ask **who owned the vehicle**, **who the driver worked for**, and **whether they were working** at the time.

## Fault systems: how the client's own share of fault counts

Very often both sides were partly at fault. The client was speeding a little; the store had a spill, but the client was looking at their phone. Each state follows one of four systems for what happens then.

### 1. Pure comparative negligence

The client's recovery is **reduced by their percentage of fault**, and they can recover **something even if they were mostly at fault**.

*Examples: California, New York.*

### 2. Modified comparative negligence: the 51% bar

The client's recovery is reduced by their percentage of fault, but if they are **more than 50% at fault (51% or more)**, they recover **nothing**. At exactly 50%, they can still recover half.

*Examples: Texas; Florida for most negligence cases arising on or after March 24, 2023 (HB 837). Florida's change does not apply to medical negligence claims, and older Florida cases were under pure comparative negligence.*

### 3. Modified comparative negligence: the 50% bar

Same idea, but the client recovers **nothing if they are 50% or more at fault**. At exactly 50%, they get nothing.

*Example: Georgia.*

### 4. Contributory negligence

The oldest and harshest rule: if the client was **even 1% at fault**, they generally recover **nothing**. Courts have some narrow exceptions (such as "last clear chance").

*Alabama, Maryland, North Carolina, Virginia and the District of Columbia.*

### Worked examples

The client's total damages are **$100,000**. The table shows what they could recover at different shares of fault under each system.

| Client's fault | Pure comparative | Modified 51% bar | Modified 50% bar | Contributory |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0% | $100,000 | $100,000 | $100,000 | $100,000 |
| 10% | $90,000 | $90,000 | $90,000 | **$0** |
| 30% | $70,000 | $70,000 | $70,000 | **$0** |
| 50% | $50,000 | $50,000 | **$0** | **$0** |
| 51% | $49,000 | **$0** | **$0** | **$0** |
| 80% | $20,000 | **$0** | **$0** | **$0** |

Look at the 50% and 51% rows: a one-point difference in fault can be the difference between $50,000 and nothing. That is why insurers fight hard over fault percentages, and why every fact that shows the client was careful matters.

**In practice** the percentages are argued by the attorney and the adjuster, decided by a jury if the case goes to trial, and they rarely look this tidy. The examples only show how the rules work.

## Joint and several liability, briefly

When **more than one defendant** is at fault, states also differ on how the damages are split among them:

- **Joint and several liability:** the client can collect the full amount from any one defendant, who then has to go after the others.
- **Several (proportionate) liability:** each defendant pays only its own share. If one defendant has no insurance or money, the client may not collect that share.
- **Mixed systems:** many states use one rule or the other depending on the defendant's share of fault or the type of damages.

Many states, Florida among them, have moved mostly to several liability. For staff, the practical point is: **identify every party who may be at fault, and every insurance policy that may cover them.** More than one at-fault party can mean more than one policy.

## Why intake asks what it asks

Each standard intake question (Days 7–9) maps to an element or to fault:

| Intake question | What it proves or disproves |
|---|---|
| How did the accident happen? Describe it step by step. | Breach; the client's own share of fault |
| Where were you, and which way were you going? What lane? | Fault (who had the right of way) |
| Was a police report made? Was anyone cited? | Breach; possible negligence per se |
| Were there witnesses? Photos? Video? Dashcam? | Independent proof of breach and fault |
| Who owned the other vehicle? Was the driver working? | Vicarious liability; more insurance |
| Were you wearing a seatbelt? Using your phone? Drinking? | The client's own share of fault (a seatbelt defense is allowed in some states) |
| When did you first get medical treatment? Where does it hurt? | Causation and damages |
| Have you had injuries to the same body parts before? Other accidents? | Causation (pre-existing conditions) |
| Did you miss work? | Economic damages |

Ask them the way the script says, write down **the caller's own words**, and don't fill in blanks with what you think happened. A detail that sounds bad for the client (they were going a little fast, they didn't see the other car) is exactly what the attorney most needs to know. A client is better served by an attorney who knows the weak spots early.

## What you must never do

- **Never tell a caller who was at fault**, or that they "definitely" have a case. "That sounds like the other driver's fault" is a legal opinion.
- **Never tell a caller they don't have a case** because they were partly at fault, or that their state "doesn't allow" a recovery. Only an attorney evaluates fault.
- **Never estimate what the case is worth** or how much fault will reduce it.
- **Never coach the caller's story.** Don't suggest facts ("so they hit you from behind, right?"). Ask open questions and record the answers.
- **Never discuss fault with the other side's insurer** (see *Opening a 3P claim: what you can and can't share with the insurer*). Questions about how the accident happened go to the attorney.

A safe line when a caller asks "Do I have a case?":

> "I can't evaluate that myself, but I'm going to get all the details to the attorney, who will review everything and let you know."

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Summarizing the caller's story in your own words | The attorney can't tell what the client actually said | Record their words, then the facts in order |
| Leaving out details that hurt the client | The attorney is surprised later, after time and money are spent | Record everything; the attorney decides what it means |
| Not asking who owned the vehicle or whether the driver was working | A second policy (an employer's) can be missed | Always ask about ownership and employment |
| Telling a caller "that's 100% their fault" | It's legal advice and can be wrong | Say the attorney will review it |
| Assuming the same fault rule applies in every state | A Virginia or Maryland case can be lost over 1% fault | Note the state; let the attorney apply its rule |
| Ignoring pre-existing injuries | Causation is attacked later | Ask about prior injuries and accidents and note them |

## Quick reference

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Negligence** | Failing to use reasonable care, causing harm |
| **Duty / breach / causation / damages** | The four elements the plaintiff must prove |
| **Proximate cause** | The harm was a foreseeable result of the careless act |
| **Negligence per se** | Breaking a safety law can establish negligence |
| **Respondeat superior** | An employer is responsible for an employee's negligence on the job |
| **Negligent entrustment** | Lending a vehicle to someone you know is unsafe |
| **Pure comparative** | Recovery reduced by the client's fault, at any percentage (e.g., California, New York) |
| **Modified 51% bar** | Nothing if the client is more than 50% at fault (e.g., Texas; Florida since March 24, 2023) |
| **Modified 50% bar** | Nothing if the client is 50% or more at fault (e.g., Georgia) |
| **Contributory negligence** | Nothing if the client is at fault at all (AL, MD, NC, VA, DC) |
| **Joint and several liability** | Any defendant can be made to pay the whole amount |

**The rules for staff:** gather the facts behind all four elements · record the caller's own words, good and bad · ask about ownership, employment, prior injuries and witnesses · never say who was at fault or whether there's a case.
