## Why deadline math matters

A missed deadline is one of the most serious mistakes a law firm can make. Miss the **statute of limitations** and the client's claim is gone for good. Miss a court deadline and the firm may lose the right to file a document, have evidence excluded, face sanctions or have the case dismissed. Missed deadlines are also one of the most common reasons lawyers are sued for malpractice.

Courts don't accept "I counted wrong" as an excuse. Every court system has a **rule for counting days**, and the counting is more precise than most people expect: which day you start on, whether weekends count, what happens when the last day is a holiday and whether extra days are added because a document was mailed.

This article builds on **Day 6: Calendar Management Training**. It explains how deadlines are counted under the **Federal Rules of Civil Procedure (FRCP) Rule 6** and under Florida's **Rule of General Practice and Judicial Administration 2.514**, with worked examples, and the docketing habits that keep a firm from missing a date.

**Your role:** you calendar deadlines, set reminders and flag problems. **The attorney decides** what a deadline is when the rule is unclear, whether to ask for an extension and which rule applies. When in doubt, ask, and never guess on the safe-looking side without telling the attorney.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Trigger event** | The event that starts the clock: service of a document, filing of an order, a hearing date, the date of the accident |
| **Calendar days** | Every day on the calendar, including weekends and holidays. Most court deadlines are counted this way |
| **Court days / business days** | Only days the court is open (excludes weekends and court holidays). Some rules use these; read the rule carefully |
| **Legal holiday** | A day defined as a holiday by the rule (federal holidays for federal court; state holidays and court closures for state court) |
| **Service** | Delivering a document to the other party. *How* it was served (mail, email, hand delivery) can change the deadline |
| **Docketing** | Entering deadlines, hearings and reminders into the firm's calendar or case management system |
| **Statute of limitations (SOL)** | The law setting the last day a lawsuit can be filed |

## Counting forward under FRCP 6(a)

For a period stated in **days** (for example "within 14 days after service"), FRCP 6(a)(1) says:

1. **Exclude the day of the trigger event.** Day 1 is the day after.
2. **Count every day**, including Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays.
3. **Include the last day.**
4. **If the last day is a Saturday, Sunday or legal holiday**, the deadline moves to the **next day that is not** a Saturday, Sunday or legal holiday.

Two more points:

- **When the last day ends.** For electronic filing, the last day ends at **midnight in the court's time zone**. For filing by other means, it ends when the clerk's office is scheduled to close (FRCP 6(a)(4)). Many firms treat the end of the business day as the real deadline to leave room for technical problems.
- **Legal holidays in federal court** (FRCP 6(a)(6)) are the federal holidays: New Year's Day, Martin Luther King Jr. Day, Washington's Birthday (Presidents' Day), Memorial Day, Juneteenth, Independence Day, Labor Day, Columbus Day, Veterans Day, Thanksgiving Day and Christmas Day, plus any day declared a holiday by the President or Congress, and (for state-law-based periods) a holiday of the state where the court is located. The day after Thanksgiving is **not** a federal legal holiday.
- **If the clerk's office is inaccessible** (for example the e-filing system is down or the court is closed for weather), FRCP 6(a)(3) can extend the deadline. Don't rely on this without the attorney's confirmation.

### Counting backward

Some deadlines are counted **back** from an event, such as "no later than 14 days before the hearing". Count backward the same way: exclude the event day, count every day. If you land on a weekend or holiday, **continue backward** to the previous day that is not a weekend or holiday (FRCP 6(a)(5)). Backward deadlines arrive *earlier*, not later.

### "Within", "after" and "before"

- **"Within 14 days after service"**: the last day to act is day 14.
- **"At least 14 days before the hearing"** or **"no later than 14 days before"**: count back 14 days; that day is the last day to act.
- **"Before" a date**: means the day before that date at the latest. If the order says "file before December 1", file by November 30.

When the wording is unusual, or an order sets its own date, the order's exact words control. Calendar the date stated in the order, and flag anything ambiguous to the attorney.

## Extra days for mail service: FRCP 6(d)

When a party must act within a time **after being served**, and the document was served **by mail** (or by leaving it with the court clerk, or by other means the party consented to), **3 days are added after the period would otherwise expire** (FRCP 6(d)).

- **Email and e-filing service (CM/ECF) get no extra days.** Electronic service was removed from Rule 6(d) in **2016**. Many older checklists still say "add 3 days for e-service". That is out of date for federal court.
- **How to apply it:** first compute the normal deadline under 6(a), *then* add 3 days. If that new date falls on a weekend or holiday, roll it forward to the next business day.
- **It applies only to deadlines triggered by service**, not to deadlines that run from the filing or entry of an order.

## Florida: Rule 2.514

Florida state courts count time under **Florida Rule of General Practice and Judicial Administration 2.514**. The counting method is very close to the federal rule:

- **Begin counting from the next day** after the trigger event.
- **Count every day**, including weekends and legal holidays.
- **Include the last day**; if it is a Saturday, Sunday or legal holiday, the period continues to the next day that is not one.
- **The last day ends** at midnight for electronic filing, or when the clerk's office closes for filing by other means.
- **Legal holidays** include the days set by statute or by the chief judge's order closing the courthouse, so a local court closure can matter. Check the court's holiday calendar.
- **Counting backward** works the same way as in federal court: if you land on a weekend or holiday, continue backward.

**The big difference is mail.** Under Rule 2.514(b), when a document is **served by mail**, **5 days** (not 3) are added after the period would otherwise expire. **Service by email** (the normal method in Florida under Rule 2.516) **adds no days**.

Other states have their own rules. Some add a different number of days for mail, and some (California, for example) count certain motion deadlines in **court days** rather than calendar days. Always check which rule applies to the court the case is in.

## Worked examples

All examples use the 2026 calendar. Check them against the court's own holiday list before relying on a similar date.

**Example 1: federal, electronic service.** A motion is served through CM/ECF on **Friday, November 6, 2026**. The response is due within **14 days after service**.

- Day 1 is Saturday, November 7. Day 14 is **Friday, November 20**.
- Electronic service: no extra days. **Deadline: Friday, November 20, 2026.**

**Example 2: the same motion served by mail (federal).**

- The normal period ends Friday, November 20.
- Add 3 days under Rule 6(d): Monday, November 23. **Deadline: Monday, November 23, 2026.**

**Example 3: last day is a holiday (federal).** A complaint is served on the defendant on **Thursday, November 5, 2026**. The answer is due **21 days after service** (FRCP 12(a)(1)(A)(i)).

- Day 21 is **Thursday, November 26**, which is Thanksgiving, a legal holiday.
- Roll forward to the next day that isn't a weekend or legal holiday. **Deadline: Friday, November 27, 2026.** (The day after Thanksgiving is not a federal holiday, but confirm the attorney wants to file that day rather than earlier.)

**Example 4: last day is a weekend (Florida).** A defendant is served with the summons and complaint on **Monday, November 2, 2026**. In Florida, the answer is due **20 days after service of process** (Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.140(a)).

- Day 20 is **Sunday, November 22**.
- Roll forward. **Deadline: Monday, November 23, 2026.**

**Example 5: Florida mail service.** Interrogatories are served on **Monday, November 9, 2026**. Answers are due within **30 days after service** (Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.340(a)).

- Served by **email**: day 30 is **Wednesday, December 9, 2026**. No extra days.
- Served by **mail**: December 9 plus 5 days under Rule 2.514(b) is **Monday, December 14, 2026**.

**Example 6: counting backward.** A hearing is set for **Monday, December 14, 2026**, and a document must be filed **at least 15 days before the hearing**.

- Counting back 15 days from December 14 lands on **Sunday, November 29**.
- Continue backward to the previous day that isn't a weekend or legal holiday: **Friday, November 27** in federal court. If the state court is closed on the day after Thanksgiving (many are), it moves back again to **Wednesday, November 25**.
- **Calendar the earlier date** and confirm with the attorney.

## Statutes of limitations start at intake

The most important deadline in a personal injury case is set the day the case is signed: the **statute of limitations**. It is not a court rule but a law, and it varies by state and by type of claim.

- **Calendar the SOL at intake**, from the date of loss, as soon as the case is opened, with reminders well in advance. Don't wait for the case to reach litigation.
- **SOLs differ by claim type.** For example, in Florida the general negligence SOL was shortened from 4 years to **2 years** for causes of action that accrued after March 24, 2023 (HB 837). Medical malpractice, wrongful death, property damage and claims against government bodies often have different periods.
- **Claims against government entities** often require a written **notice of claim** within a short period, sometimes months, before suit can be filed. Flag any case involving a city bus, a government vehicle, a public building or a public road to the attorney right away.
- **Special rules can shorten or extend the SOL**, such as the client being a minor, the date the injury was discovered or the defendant leaving the state. These are legal questions. The attorney calculates and confirms the SOL; you calendar what the attorney confirms and flag anything that might change it.
- **Insurance policies have their own deadlines** too, such as PIP notice requirements or UM claim deadlines. Calendar those as the attorney or SOP directs.

## Docketing best practices

1. **Calendar from the document, never from memory.** Read the order, notice or rule; note the trigger date, the method of service and the rule that applies.
2. **Calendar it the same day** the document arrives.
3. **Record how you computed it** in the calendar entry or case note: "Response due 11/23/2026 = 14 days after mail service 11/06 (FRCP 6(a)) + 3 days (6(d))." This lets someone else check your work.
4. **Two-person check.** A second person (another VA, the paralegal or the attorney) independently computes every court deadline and every SOL. If you get different dates, use the earlier one until the attorney decides.
5. **Set reminders before the deadline**, not just on it. A common pattern is **30, 14, 7 and 1 days** before, plus the due date itself. Firms with longer projects (such as an SOL) add reminders months ahead.
6. **Calendar to the right people.** The responsible attorney, the paralegal or case manager, and anyone else the firm's procedure lists.
7. **Use the firm's system**, not a personal calendar or sticky note.
8. **Check court holiday calendars** at the start of each year and when a closure is announced.
9. **Recalculate when something changes.** A continued hearing, an amended order or an extension changes the dependent deadlines. Update all of them and note why.
10. **Never delete a deadline** without the attorney's instruction. Mark it completed or moved, and note who approved the change.

## When a deadline is unclear

- **Stop and ask the attorney** before calendaring it, and put a placeholder on the **earliest possible date** in the meantime.
- **Say exactly what is unclear:** "The notice was served by both email and mail. Under 2.514, email adds no days, so I've calendared 12/09 rather than 12/14. Please confirm."
- **Never pick the later date to be safe.** The later date is the dangerous one.
- **Never tell a client or opposing party what a deadline is** unless the attorney has confirmed it and asked you to.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting the trigger day as day 1.** Day 1 is the next day.
- **Skipping weekends** when the rule counts calendar days.
- **Adding 3 days for email service** in federal court (removed in 2016), or adding 3 instead of 5 for mail in Florida.
- **Adding the mail days before rolling a weekend**, instead of computing the normal deadline first and then adding.
- **Rolling a backward deadline forward** instead of backward.
- **Forgetting state court closures** that aren't federal holidays.
- **Calendaring from an email summary** instead of from the order itself.
- **Not calendaring the SOL at intake.**
- **Only one reminder**, on the due date itself.
- **Not recording how the date was computed**, so no one can check it.

## Quick reference

| Question | Federal court (FRCP 6) | Florida state court (Rule 2.514) |
|---|---|---|
| Count the trigger day? | No, start the next day | No, start the next day |
| Count weekends and holidays? | Yes, every day | Yes, every day |
| Last day on a weekend or holiday? | Move to the next day that isn't | Move to the next day that isn't |
| Counting backward lands on a weekend? | Move to the previous day that isn't | Move to the previous day that isn't |
| Extra days for mail service | **+3 days** (Rule 6(d)) | **+5 days** (Rule 2.514(b)) |
| Extra days for email / e-service | **None** (since 2016) | **None** |
| When does the last day end? | Midnight (court's time zone) for e-filing; clerk's closing for other filing | Midnight for e-filing; clerk's closing for other filing |
| Legal holidays | Federal holidays (incl. Juneteenth), days declared by the President or Congress, state holidays for state-law periods | Statutory holidays and days the court is closed by order |
| Docketing habit | Calendar from the document · record the math · second check · reminders 30/14/7/1 days · ask when unclear | Same |
