## Why security is part of your job

A personal injury file holds some of the most sensitive information a person has: their Social Security number, date of birth, medical records, health insurance, bank details for settlement payments and the story of the worst day of their life. Criminals know law firms hold this information, and they know firms move large sums of money at settlement. That makes law firms, and the remote staff who work for them, a favorite target.

Lawyers have an ethical duty to protect it. ABA Model Rule 1.6(c) requires a lawyer to make reasonable efforts to prevent unauthorized access to client information, and Rule 5.3 makes the lawyer responsible for how staff handle it (see *Legal ethics rules every non-lawyer VA must know*). Medical records are also protected health information, and many states have data-breach laws that require notifying clients if their data is exposed.

As a remote VA, you are often the first line of defense. Most breaches don't start with a hacker breaking through a firewall. They start with **one person clicking one link, or trusting one email**. This article covers the threats you will actually meet and the habits that stop them.

## The threats at a glance

| Threat | What it looks like | Your defense |
|---|---|---|
| **Phishing** | An email or text that tricks you into clicking a link, opening a file or typing a password | Check the sender and the link before you click; when in doubt, report |
| **Business email compromise (BEC)** | An email that appears to come from the attorney, a client or a vendor asking you to pay, change bank details or send files | Verify by phone at a number you already have |
| **Settlement wire fraud** | "New" wiring instructions for a settlement or lien payoff | Never change payment details from an email alone |
| **Credential theft** | A fake login page for email, the case system or a document portal | Use a password manager and multi-factor authentication |
| **Unsafe devices and networks** | A shared family computer, public Wi‑Fi, an unlocked screen | Dedicated work device, VPN, lock your screen |
| **Data leaks** | Client files sent to the wrong person, saved to a personal account or pasted into an outside tool | Double-check recipients; use only approved tools |

## Phishing: stop before you click

Phishing messages are designed to make you act fast. They often look like they come from a court e-filing system, a records company, a bank, the firm's IT team, Microsoft or Google, a delivery service or an attorney at the firm.

**Warning signs:**

- **Urgency or pressure:** "Your account will be closed today", "The attorney needs this in 10 minutes", "Final notice".
- **A sender address that doesn't quite match:** `jsmith@lawfirm-legal.com` instead of `jsmith@lawfirm.com`, or a free email address for a company that should have its own.
- **A link that goes somewhere unexpected.** Hover over a link (or press and hold on a phone) to see the real address before you click.
- **Unexpected attachments**, especially ZIP files, macro-enabled Office files, or "invoices" and "court notices" you weren't expecting.
- **A request to log in** through the email's link. Go to the site directly instead.
- **Unusual requests:** gift cards, changes to payroll or bank details, or "keep this between us".

**The habit:** if a message asks you to click, log in, pay or send something, pause and check. If you are unsure, don't click. Report it.

## Business email compromise and wire fraud

This is the most expensive scam targeting law firms. A criminal either takes over a real email account (the attorney's, a client's, a lien holder's or a vendor's) or creates one that looks almost identical. They watch the conversation, then step in at the right moment, usually when money is about to move.

Common versions in a PI firm:

- **"Updated wiring instructions"** from a lien holder, medical provider or defense firm just before a payoff.
- **A client "asking"** for their settlement share to go to a new bank account.
- **The "attorney" emailing** a VA to buy gift cards or pay an urgent invoice.
- **A fake vendor invoice** for records, process service or court reporting, with new payment details.

**The rule: verify by phone, at a number you already have.** Not the number in the email, not the number in the signature of the new email, but the number already in the case file, on the provider's official website or in the firm's vendor list. Ask the person to confirm the request and the details.

Other protections:

- **Never change payment details based on an email alone**, even if it comes from a known address.
- **Watch for small changes** in a familiar address, a reply-to address that differs from the sender or a thread that suddenly changes tone.
- **Follow the firm's payment approval steps** every time, even if the request seems to come from a senior attorney. A real attorney will not be upset that you checked.

## Passwords and multi-factor authentication

- **Use a unique password for every account.** If one site is breached, criminals try the same password everywhere.
- **Use a password manager** if the firm provides or approves one. It remembers strong passwords so you don't have to.
- **Long beats complicated.** A passphrase of four or more random words is strong and easy to type.
- **Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA)** wherever it's offered: email, the case management system, cloud storage, e-filing portals.
- **Never approve an MFA prompt you didn't start.** If your phone asks "Are you trying to sign in?" and you aren't, deny it and report it. Someone has your password.
- **Never share passwords or MFA codes**, even with a coworker or someone who says they're from IT. Real IT staff don't need your password.
- **Don't write passwords** on sticky notes, in a notebook next to the computer or in an unprotected document.

## Your device and your workspace

**The device:**

- **Use a dedicated work device** if possible. If you must use a personal computer, use a separate user account for work that no one else in the household uses.
- **Keep it updated.** Install operating system, browser and security updates promptly; most attacks use flaws that already have a fix.
- **Run the antivirus or security software** the firm requires, and don't turn it off.
- **Don't install unapproved software**, browser extensions or "free" tools on a work device.
- **Encrypt the device** (BitLocker on Windows, FileVault on Mac) if the firm requires or allows it.

**The network:**

- **Avoid public Wi‑Fi** (cafés, airports, hotels) for client work. If you have no choice, use the firm's VPN or your phone's personal hotspot.
- **Secure your home Wi‑Fi** with a strong password and change the router's default admin password.

**The workspace:**

- **Lock your screen every time you step away** (Windows key + L on Windows; Control + Command + Q on a Mac).
- **Clean desk, clean screen.** Don't leave printed client documents out, and position your screen so others in the house or a co-working space can't read it.
- **Use headphones on calls** so others nearby can't hear client details.
- **Shred** any paper that contains client information. Don't throw it in household trash.

## Screenshots, personal accounts and AI tools

Many leaks are accidental and come from convenience.

- **Don't save client files to personal storage**, such as a personal Google Drive, Dropbox, iCloud, USB drive or your phone's photo gallery.
- **Don't forward work email to a personal email account**, even to work on it later.
- **Don't take screenshots or photos of client information** unless the task requires it, and then only into the firm's systems. Delete local copies.
- **Don't share client details in personal chat apps** (WhatsApp, Messenger, Viber) or group chats, even with other VAs, unless the firm has approved that tool for work.
- **Don't paste client information into public AI tools** or translation and grammar websites that aren't approved by the firm. What you paste may be stored, used to train the tool or seen by others. If the firm has an approved AI tool with a proper agreement, follow its rules for what can go in.
- **Check recipients before you hit send.** Autocomplete fills in the wrong "John" all the time. Sending a medical record to the wrong person is a breach.

## If something goes wrong: report it immediately

You will not be in trouble for reporting a mistake quickly. You may be in serious trouble for hiding one. Time matters: a password changed in five minutes can stop an attack that would otherwise spread through the firm.

**If you clicked a suspicious link or opened an attachment:**

1. **Stop.** Don't enter any more information.
2. **Disconnect from the internet** (turn off Wi‑Fi or unplug the cable) if you think something installed.
3. **Report it right away** to your supervisor or the firm's IT contact, by phone or the firm's reporting channel. Say what you clicked and when.
4. **Change your password** from a different, safe device if IT tells you to.
5. **Don't delete the email** until IT says so; they may need it.

**If you sent client information to the wrong person:** tell your supervisor immediately. The attorney decides what to do (for example, asking the recipient to delete it and confirm in writing). Don't try to cover it up.

**If your device is lost or stolen:** report it the same day so IT can lock or wipe it and reset your accounts.

**If you approved an MFA prompt or gave out a code by mistake:** report it at once. Treat the account as compromised.

## Common mistakes

- **Trusting a familiar name** in the "From" line without checking the actual address.
- **Calling the number in a suspicious email** to "verify" it, which reaches the scammer.
- **Reusing one password** across work and personal accounts.
- **Approving an MFA prompt** just to make it go away.
- **Working from a shared family computer** without a separate account, or leaving it unlocked.
- **Forwarding files to personal email** "just for tonight".
- **Pasting medical records into a free AI or translation tool.**
- **Waiting to report** a click or a misdirected email because you're embarrassed.

## Quick reference

| Situation | Do | Don't |
|---|---|---|
| Unexpected link or attachment | Hover to check, verify with the sender, report if unsure | Click or open it to see what it is |
| Email asks to change payment or bank details | Call a known number to verify; follow approval steps | Act on the email alone |
| "Attorney" asks for gift cards or urgent payment | Confirm by phone or through another channel | Buy or pay anything |
| MFA prompt you didn't start | Deny it and report it | Approve it |
| Working away from home | Use the VPN or a personal hotspot | Use open public Wi‑Fi |
| Stepping away from the desk | Lock the screen | Leave it open |
| Need to work on a file later | Use the firm's systems remotely | Forward it to personal email or save it to USB |
| Want help rewriting text | Use only firm-approved tools | Paste client data into public AI sites |
| Clicked something or sent to the wrong person | Report immediately | Hide it or wait |
| Device lost or stolen | Report the same day | Wait to see if it turns up |
