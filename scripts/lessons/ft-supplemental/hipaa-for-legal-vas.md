## HIPAA for legal VAs: what it is, what it means for the firm, and how you handle health information

Almost every personal injury case runs on medical records and bills. During Days 13–16 (Provider Communication Training) you learn to request them, follow up on them and verify balances. Every one of those tasks touches **protected health information (PHI)**, and the law that sets the rules for it is **HIPAA**, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996, with its Privacy Rule (45 CFR Part 164, Subpart E) and Security Rule (Subpart C).

This page explains who HIPAA applies to, why a provider will refuse a request that isn't right, what makes an authorization valid, how fast providers must answer, which records need extra permission, and how you, as a VA, keep health information safe.

## 1. Who HIPAA applies to

HIPAA applies directly to **covered entities** and their **business associates**.

| Who | Examples | Does HIPAA apply directly? |
|---|---|---|
| **Health care providers** that bill electronically | Hospitals, doctors, chiropractors, physical therapists, imaging centers, pharmacies | Yes: they are covered entities |
| **Health plans** | Health insurers, HMOs, Medicare, Medicaid, employer group health plans | Yes: covered entities |
| **Health care clearinghouses** | Companies that process billing data between providers and plans | Yes: covered entities |
| **Business associates** | Companies that handle PHI *for* a covered entity: billing companies, records-copy services (such as ChartSwap or a provider's release-of-information vendor), IT vendors | Yes, through a business associate agreement (BAA) and the law itself |
| **A personal injury law firm** representing the patient | The firm you support | **Usually no**: it is not a covered entity |

### Why the firm is usually not a covered entity

A law firm that represents an injured client gets the client's records **because the client authorized it**. The firm is not providing care or paying claims, and it is not doing work on behalf of the provider. So HIPAA usually does not apply to the firm directly.

**That does not make health information less protected at the firm.** The firm's duties come from other rules:

- **Confidentiality** (ABA Model Rule 1.6 and each state's version): everything about the client's case, health included, is confidential.
- **Duty to supervise staff** (Model Rule 5.3): the attorney must make sure you protect client information.
- **State privacy and data-breach laws**, which often cover medical and identity information.
- **Contracts and protective orders**: in litigation, a court order or a HIPAA qualified protective order may limit who can see the records and require their return or destruction at the end of the case.

### When a firm *can* be a business associate

A firm can be a business associate when it does legal work **for** a covered entity (for example, defending a hospital and receiving its patients' records). Most LSH work is for plaintiff firms, but if you ever support a firm that represents providers or health plans, expect a BAA and stricter HIPAA rules.

**The practical rule for you:** treat every piece of health information as if HIPAA applied to you. It is the standard the providers hold you to, and it is what the attorney expects.

## 2. What counts as PHI

PHI is **individually identifiable health information**: anything about a person's past, present or future physical or mental health, the care they received, or payment for that care, when it can be tied to that person.

| PHI | Not PHI (on its own) |
|---|---|
| Medical records, imaging reports, test results | A general article about back injuries |
| Bills, itemized statements, ledgers, EOBs | A provider's address and phone number |
| A diagnosis mentioned in a case note | Statistics with no names or identifiers |
| An appointment date at a named clinic | |
| The client's name **together with** a treatment detail | |

The **18 HIPAA identifiers** (name, address details smaller than a state, dates other than the year, phone, fax, email, SSN, medical record number, health plan number, account number, license numbers, vehicle identifiers including plates and VINs, device identifiers, URLs, IP addresses, biometrics, full-face photos and any other unique number or code) are what make health information "identifiable." Remove them and the information is de-identified. You will rarely de-identify anything, but the list is a good reminder of how much a single document can reveal.

## 3. The authorization: why providers accept or reject your request

A provider can release records to a third party (the firm) only with a **valid HIPAA authorization** signed by the patient or the patient's personal representative (or under another permitted route, such as a subpoena with the right assurances or a court order). This is why the intake packet has a HIPAA authorization, and why a records request goes out with a copy.

Under **45 CFR 164.508(c)**, a valid authorization must contain these **core elements**:

| # | Element | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **A description of the information** to be used or disclosed, specific and meaningful | "All medical records and bills from [date of accident] to present" is specific; a blank is not |
| 2 | **Who may disclose** it (the person or class of persons) | "Any health care provider that treated me" or a named provider |
| 3 | **Who may receive** it | The law firm by name |
| 4 | **The purpose** of the disclosure | "At the request of the individual" is enough when the patient starts it; most firm forms say "legal representation / personal injury claim" |
| 5 | **An expiration date or event** | A date, or an event such as "conclusion of my claim" |
| 6 | **The signature and date** of the patient | If a personal representative signs, a description of their authority (parent, guardian, power of attorney, estate representative) |

It must also contain these **required statements**:

- **The right to revoke** the authorization in writing, and how to do it (or a reference to the provider's privacy notice).
- **No conditioning:** the provider cannot make treatment, payment or enrollment depend on signing it (with narrow exceptions).
- **Redisclosure:** information disclosed under the authorization may be redisclosed by the recipient and may no longer be protected by HIPAA.

It must be written in **plain language**, and the patient must get a copy.

### Why requests get rejected

Providers and copy services reject authorizations every day. The common reasons:

- It is **expired** (the date passed, or it was signed so long ago that the provider's policy treats it as stale).
- It is **missing a signature or date**, or the signature doesn't match.
- The **firm's name is wrong or missing** in the "who may receive" box.
- A **personal representative** signed with no proof of authority (letters of administration, guardianship papers, POA).
- The request asks for **specially protected records** (see section 5) and the authorization doesn't cover them.
- The provider requires **its own form**.

When a request is rejected, note why, tell your case manager or attorney, and get a corrected authorization signed through the firm's process. **Never change a signed authorization yourself**: no new dates, no added provider names, no filled-in blanks after signature.

## 4. How fast providers must respond, and what they can charge

There are two different routes for getting records, and they have different rules.

### Route 1: the patient's right of access (45 CFR 164.524)

A patient has the right to see and get copies of their own records, and to direct a copy to a third party they choose.

- **Timing:** the provider must act **within 30 calendar days** of the request. It may take **one 30-day extension** if it tells the patient in writing, within the first 30 days, why and when it will respond.
- **Fees:** only a **reasonable, cost-based fee** (labor for copying, supplies, postage). The provider cannot charge search or retrieval fees for the patient's own access request.
- **Form:** the copy should be in the form the patient asks for if it is readily producible (electronic if kept electronically).

Many state laws set shorter deadlines; where state law gives the patient faster access, the provider must follow it.

### Route 2: an authorization for a third party (the firm's usual request)

When the firm sends a request with the client's **HIPAA authorization**, the provider is making a disclosure to a third party. The 30-day access deadline and the cost-based fee limit do not automatically apply to this kind of request. **Fees are generally set by state law** (many states cap per-page and certification fees) and by the provider's or copy service's policies. That is why a copy service can send an invoice before releasing records.

Federal guidance on how fees apply when a patient *directs* records to a third party has changed over time and been litigated, so follow the firm's procedure on whether to request records as a patient-directed access request or as an authorized disclosure. Your case manager will tell you which route the firm uses.

**For your follow-ups:** calendar the follow-up when you send the request, follow the firm's follow-up schedule (Days 13–16), and note every call, invoice and promised date.

## 5. Records that need extra permission

Some records have stronger protection than ordinary medical records. A general authorization may **not** be enough.

| Record type | Rule | What it means for your request |
|---|---|---|
| **Psychotherapy notes** | Under HIPAA, a therapist's separate personal notes from counseling sessions need a **separate, specific authorization** (45 CFR 164.508(a)(2)). They are different from the regular mental health record (diagnosis, medications, session dates, treatment plan) | Ask the attorney before requesting them; they are rarely needed and often sensitive |
| **Substance use disorder treatment records** from federally assisted programs | Protected by **42 CFR Part 2**, which requires a Part 2–compliant consent and limits redisclosure. The rules were revised in 2024 to align more closely with HIPAA, but Part 2 consent and redisclosure requirements still apply | Use the firm's Part 2 consent form if one exists; flag these records to the attorney |
| **HIV/AIDS, genetic testing, sexually transmitted infection, mental health** records | Many **states** require specific consent language for these | Check whether the firm's authorization includes the required state language |
| **Minors' records** | A parent or guardian usually signs as personal representative, but some states let minors consent to certain care (for example reproductive or mental health care) and control those records | Get the signature from the right person; flag anything unusual |
| **Deceased patients** | The **personal representative of the estate** (executor or administrator) signs, with proof such as letters of administration. HIPAA protects a deceased person's PHI for 50 years after death | Ask for the estate documents before requesting |

## 6. The minimum necessary mindset

HIPAA's **minimum necessary** standard requires covered entities to use and disclose only the PHI needed for the purpose. It doesn't technically apply to disclosures the patient authorized, and the firm usually needs the full injury-related record. But as a working habit, apply it to everything you do with the information:

- **Share only what the task needs.** An adjuster on an opening call gets no medical details (see *Opening a 3P claim: what you can and can't share with the insurer*). Medical records go out with the demand, when the attorney decides.
- **Request what the case needs.** Usually records and bills from the date of the accident (or a period before it, if the attorney asks for prior records) for the injuries in the claim.
- **Don't repeat diagnoses in places they don't belong**: team chat, email subject lines, calendar invites, file names visible to people outside the case.
- **Talk about health details only with people working on the case**, and never in a public place or on a call others can overhear.

## 7. Handling PHI day to day

### Sending and receiving

- **Use the firm's secure channels:** the case management system, the firm's secure portal, encrypted email, e-fax, or the provider's or copy service's portal.
- **Verify the fax number** before you send anything. Call the provider or check its official records department page. A records department number found online can be out of date.
- **Use a cover sheet** with a confidentiality notice on every fax.
- **Double-check the recipient** before you click send. Autocomplete in email is the most common way PHI goes to the wrong person.
- **Never send unencrypted PHI** by regular email unless the firm's policy allows it and the recipient is verified. Never use personal email, personal phone texts or personal cloud storage for client records.

### Storing

- Save records **only in the case file** in the firm's system, named by the firm's naming convention (see *Naming Documents and Case Folders*).
- **Do not download** records to your personal computer, and delete any local copy you had to make once it is saved where it belongs.
- **Lock your screen** whenever you step away, and work where others can't see your screen.
- **Never print** at home unless the firm's policy allows it, and shred anything you print.

### Talking on the phone

- **Verify who you are speaking to** before discussing anything about a client's care. Confirm you have the right provider and the right department.
- When a provider calls asking for a client's information, confirm the provider is on the case and follow the firm's call procedure.
- **Never leave health details in a voicemail.** Leave your name, the firm's name and a callback number.

## 8. When something goes wrong

Mistakes happen: a fax to the wrong number, records attached to the wrong case, a provider sending you another patient's records. What matters is that you **report it right away**.

| What happened | What to do |
|---|---|
| **You sent PHI to the wrong person or place** | Stop. Tell your supervisor or attorney **immediately**. Do not try to fix it quietly. The firm decides whether to contact the recipient, ask for deletion and whether notice is required under state breach laws |
| **A provider sent you records for someone who isn't your client** | Don't read further, don't forward them, don't save them to a case. Tell your supervisor; the firm will notify the provider and return or destroy them as instructed |
| **Records are in the wrong case file** | Tell your case manager; move them as instructed and note the correction |
| **You think your account or device was compromised** | Report it at once to your supervisor and the firm's IT contact |

Covered entities have breach-notification duties under HIPAA, and many states have data-breach laws that may apply to the firm. Those decisions are the firm's. **Your job is to report quickly and accurately.** A reported mistake can usually be contained; a hidden one can't.

## 9. Your role and its limits

**You do:** prepare and send records requests with the signed authorization, track them, follow up, log invoices, save records correctly, protect PHI in every step, and report problems.

**You don't:** decide whether a record is privileged or must be produced in discovery, alter an authorization, decide what medical information goes to an insurer, or give anyone legal advice about HIPAA. Those decisions belong to the attorney.

## Common mistakes

- Sending a request with an **expired, unsigned or incomplete** authorization.
- Editing a signed authorization (adding a provider, changing a date) instead of getting a new one signed.
- Asking for **psychotherapy notes or substance use treatment records** with a general authorization.
- Faxing to a number that wasn't verified.
- Writing the client's diagnosis in an **email subject line, calendar invite or team chat**.
- Saving records to a personal device or personal email.
- Leaving medical details in a **voicemail**.
- Thinking "HIPAA doesn't apply to the firm" means the information isn't protected.
- Not reporting a misdirected fax or email because it seems small.
