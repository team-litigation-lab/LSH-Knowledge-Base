*A guide for case managers and claims staff. Insurance rules, minimum limits and deadlines differ by state and by policy. Use this as a framework, read the actual policy, and confirm anything unclear with the handling attorney.*

## 1. Why coverage comes first

In a personal injury claim, the money for the client usually comes from insurance, not from the at-fault person's pocket. A strong case with no coverage behind it can be worth far less than an average case with a large policy. That is why coverage is checked at intake, again when a claim is opened, and again before demanding or settling.

Three questions drive everything:

1. **Who is responsible?** (liability: who was at fault, and who else may share responsibility)
2. **What insurance applies?** (every policy that could pay: theirs, ours, the employer's, an umbrella)
3. **How much is available, and in what order?** (limits, deductibles, offsets and who pays first)

## 2. Insurance basics every case manager should know

| Term | What it means in a claim |
|---|---|
| **Insurer (carrier)** | The company that issued the policy and will pay or deny the claim. |
| **Policyholder / named insured** | The person or business who bought the policy. |
| **Insured** | Anyone the policy covers: the named insured, resident family members, and often other people who drive the car with permission (permissive users). |
| **Policy period** | The dates the policy was in force. The accident date must fall inside it. |
| **Premium** | The price paid for the policy. A lapse for non-payment can end coverage. |
| **Limits** | The most the insurer will pay for a covered claim. |
| **Deductible** | The part the insured pays before the policy pays. It mostly applies to first-party property coverage such as collision. |
| **Endorsement (rider)** | A change added to the policy that adds, removes or changes coverage. |
| **Exclusion** | A situation the policy does not cover, such as using a personal car for delivery work or an excluded driver. |
| **Declarations page (dec page)** | The summary page of the policy: insured names, vehicles, coverage types, limits, deductibles and policy dates. It is the first document to ask for. |
| **Claim number / policy number** | The numbers to put on every letter, call and email to the carrier. |

### First-party and third-party claims (1P and 3P)

- **First-party (1P):** a claim against *our own client's* insurance company, such as PIP, MedPay, UM/UIM or collision. The client has a contract with that insurer. Coverage and limits are usually easy to confirm and the adjuster will confirm them quickly.
- **Third-party (3P):** a claim against *the at-fault person's* insurance company, such as bodily injury (BI) liability and property damage (PD). The client has no contract with that insurer. The adjuster often does not confirm coverage right away, asks for more information first and runs their own investigation.

Knowing which kind of claim you are opening tells you who to contact, what to expect and what to ask for.

## 3. The coverages you will see in injury cases

### 3.1 Liability coverage (pays for harm the insured causes to others)

- **Bodily injury (BI) liability** pays for injuries the insured causes to other people: medical bills, lost income, pain and suffering. It also pays the insured's legal defense if they are sued. This is the main coverage in most third-party injury claims.
- **Property damage (PD) liability** pays for damage the insured causes to other people's property, mainly vehicles.

**How limits are written.**
- *Split limits*, for example **25/50/25** (written $25,000 / $50,000 / $25,000): $25,000 per person for bodily injury, $50,000 total per accident for bodily injury, and $25,000 for property damage.
- *Combined single limit (CSL)*, for example **$100,000 CSL**: one total amount for all injury and property damage from one accident, however it is divided.

State minimums are often low. For example, Nevada's minimum is 25/50/20 (confirm the current figure for the state of the accident). Many at-fault drivers carry only the minimum, which is often less than the injuries are worth. That is why the other coverages below matter.

### 3.2 Uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage (UM/UIM)

- **Uninsured motorist (UM)** protects our client's own policy when the at-fault driver has *no* insurance, or flees the scene (hit and run).
- **Underinsured motorist (UIM)** applies when the at-fault driver has insurance, but *not enough* to cover the client's damages.

UM/UIM is bought on the client's *own* policy (and sometimes can be used under a household member's policy), so it is a first-party claim. Points to check:

- **Limits and "stacking".** Some states allow combining UM/UIM limits from several vehicles or policies. Others do not.
- **Offset or add-on.** In some states UIM pays only the difference between the damages and the at-fault driver's limits, up to the UIM limit. In others it is added on top of the at-fault driver's payment. The policy and state law decide.
- **Notice and consent.** Many policies require notifying the UIM insurer *before* accepting the at-fault driver's limits, and getting its consent or waiver, so the UIM claim is not lost. Missing this step can forfeit the coverage. Ask the attorney before any limits settlement.
- **Hit and run.** Many states require a prompt police report, and sometimes physical contact with the other vehicle, to make a UM claim.
- **Time limits.** UM/UIM claims can have their own contract deadlines, sometimes shorter than the statute of limitations. Calendar them.

### 3.3 Medical payments coverage (MedPay)

Optional coverage on the client's own auto policy that pays medical bills for the insured and passengers *regardless of fault*, up to a limit (often $1,000 to $10,000). It usually pays quickly, can cover deductibles and co-pays, and often does not require proof of fault. Check whether the policy lets the insurer be repaid from the settlement (reimbursement) or not.

### 3.4 Personal injury protection (PIP) and no-fault

In *no-fault* states, the client's own PIP coverage pays medical bills and often part of lost income first, whoever caused the crash. A claim against the at-fault driver is allowed only after a threshold is met (a dollar amount of medical bills, or a type of serious injury, depending on the state). In other states, PIP is optional and works much like MedPay but with broader benefits. PIP is a first-party claim, so the adjuster confirms coverage quickly.

### 3.5 Property coverages

- **Collision** pays to repair or replace the client's own vehicle after a crash, whoever is at fault, less the deductible. It is mandatory if the car is financed or leased.
- **Comprehensive** pays for non-crash damage such as theft, vandalism, weather, fire or hitting an animal.
- **Rental / loss of use** pays for a substitute vehicle while the car is repaired. If the other driver is at fault, loss of use can be claimed from their insurer.
- **Total loss and diminished value.** If repair costs exceed a set share of the car's value, the car is a total loss and the insurer pays its value. A repaired car can still lose market value (diminished value), which can be claimed from the at-fault party's insurer in many states.

### 3.6 Umbrella and excess coverage

An **umbrella** or **excess** policy adds liability coverage on top of the underlying auto or home policy, often $1 million or more. It applies only after the primary limits are paid. It is easy to miss, so always ask whether the at-fault person or business has one.

### 3.7 Coverage tied to business and work

- **Commercial auto and business policies.** If the at-fault driver was working (delivering, driving a company vehicle, or driving a truck), the employer's policy may apply, often with much higher limits. Federal law sets higher minimum limits for many trucks. The employer can also be responsible for the employee's negligence (vicarious liability, or *respondeat superior*).
- **Hired and non-owned auto coverage.** Covers a business when its employees drive their own or rented cars for work.
- **Workers' compensation.** If the client was hurt while working, workers' compensation pays medical bills and part of lost wages whoever caused the crash, but it generally prevents suing the employer. A separate claim against a *third party* who caused the injury may still be possible, and the workers' compensation insurer will have a lien on it.
- **Rideshare (Uber, Lyft and similar).** Coverage depends on the driver's app status: *app off* (the driver's personal policy), *app on and waiting for a ride* (a lower company policy), and *accepted a ride or carrying a passenger* (typically much higher company liability coverage, often $1 million). The status at the moment of the crash must be documented.

### 3.8 Property and premises coverage

- **Homeowners and renters policies** include liability coverage for injuries on the property and, often, *medical payments to others* without proving fault.
- **Commercial general liability (CGL)** covers businesses for customer and visitor injuries (slip and fall, for example).
- **Landlord, property manager and contractor policies** may also apply. Ask who owned, leased, maintained and controlled the property.

### 3.9 The client's health insurance and other benefits

These are *not* sources of recovery from the at-fault party, but they affect the case because they pay bills, and often have repayment rights from the settlement:

- **Private health insurance and ERISA plans.** May claim reimbursement. Self-funded ERISA plans can have strong rights, subject to the plan terms.
- **Medicare and Medicaid.** Have legal rights to be repaid for accident-related care (conditional payments and Medicaid liens). Report them early.
- **Short- and long-term disability insurance.** May pay lost income and may have repayment rights.
- **Provider liens and letters of protection (LOP).** Providers who treat on a promise of payment from the settlement.

## 4. Who pays first? How the coverages fit together

Coverages are usually used in a sensible order. The exact order depends on the state and the policies, but a common pattern for a car crash is:

1. **MedPay or PIP** (the client's own policy) pays early medical bills, regardless of fault.
2. **The client's health insurance** may pay bills that MedPay/PIP does not cover (and will want repayment).
3. **The at-fault driver's BI liability** is the main source for the injury claim, up to its limits.
4. **UIM** (client's own policy) pays the gap if the at-fault limits are not enough.
5. **Umbrella/excess** or **an employer's policy** adds more if available.
6. **Property:** the at-fault driver's PD liability pays for the car, or the client uses collision and the insurer later seeks repayment.

### A worked example

A client's medical bills and other damages are valued at $120,000. The at-fault driver carries 25/50/25. The client has MedPay of $5,000 and UIM of $100,000/$300,000.

| Source | Amount | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| MedPay (client's policy) | $5,000 | Paid early; check for any reimbursement right. |
| At-fault BI liability | $25,000 | The per-person limit is the most this policy pays. |
| UIM (client's policy) | Up to the gap | Pays the difference between the damages and the at-fault limits, up to $100,000, under an offset-type policy. Prior notice and consent were needed before taking the $25,000. |
| Remaining shortfall | Depends | If damages still exceed everything, ask whether an umbrella, an employer policy or other coverage exists. |

The point of the example is the *process*: find every coverage, check the limits and the rules of each, and sequence the claims so no coverage is lost.

## 5. The coverage hunt: how to find every policy

Work through this checklist and document each answer in the case management system.

**At intake, ask the client:**
- Who owned and who drove each vehicle in the crash? Was anyone working or driving for an app?
- What auto insurance does the client have? Collision? MedPay or PIP? UM/UIM? What limits?
- Does anyone in the household have a separate auto policy? Any umbrella policy?
- What health insurance, Medicare, Medicaid or disability coverage does the client have?
- Was the client at work or on a work errand?

**About the at-fault side:**
- The at-fault driver's insurer, policy number and claim number (from the crash report or exchange of information).
- The *owner* of the at-fault vehicle, if different from the driver. Their policy may also apply.
- The driver's employer and whether the driver was working.
- Other possible defendants: a property owner, a business, a vehicle owner, a government body.

**How to confirm coverage:**
- Send the **letter of representation (LOR)** to every insurer. Ask the adjuster to confirm coverage, policy limits and any other insurance, and to send the declarations page. Some states require insurers to disclose limits on request, and others do not.
- Ask the at-fault party's lawyer or insurer for any **umbrella or excess** policy.
- Get the confirmation **in writing**. A verbal answer is not enough.
- In a lawsuit, **discovery** allows requests for all insurance policies that may cover the claim, and the federal rules require disclosure of insurance agreements.
- Note **reservation of rights letters** (the insurer pays under conditions) and **denials**, and send them to the attorney right away.

## 6. Reading a declarations page: a quick checklist

- Are the **named insured** and the **driver/vehicle** correct?
- Is the **accident date** inside the **policy period**?
- Is the **vehicle listed** (or covered as a replacement or a newly acquired vehicle)?
- Which **coverages** are shown, and what are the **limits** for each?
- Are there **endorsements** that add or remove coverage?
- Are there **excluded drivers** or business-use limits?
- Is the policy **personal or commercial**?

If the dec page and the facts do not match (wrong driver, unlisted vehicle, lapsed policy), tell the attorney. These are the situations where coverage is disputed.

## 7. Common coverage problems and how to respond

| Problem | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **No insurance / lapsed policy** | The at-fault driver had no coverage on the date. | Check the client's UM coverage, other responsible parties (owner, employer) and the attorney's advice on collecting from the person. |
| **Low limits** | The at-fault policy is far below the damages. | Look for UIM, umbrella, employer or owner coverage. Follow the notice and consent rules before accepting limits. |
| **Reservation of rights** | The insurer is investigating whether it must cover the claim. | Alert the attorney. Keep records of every notice and date. |
| **Coverage denial** | The insurer says the claim is not covered (excluded driver, intentional act, business use). | Get the denial in writing. The attorney decides whether to challenge it. |
| **Late notice** | A policy requires prompt notice and it was not given. | Give notice immediately to every possibly responsible insurer, and document it. |
| **Permissive use** | The driver was not the owner. Coverage for permissive users depends on the policy and state. | Identify the owner's policy and any policy on the driver. |
| **Shared fault / multiple defendants** | Several people may share responsibility. | Open claims against each, and track every policy and limit. |
| **Government vehicle** | Different notice rules, short deadlines and caps may apply. | Calendar the notice-of-claim deadline immediately and tell the attorney. |
| **Unreasonable refusal to pay** | The insurer delays or refuses a clearly valid claim. | Document everything. The attorney decides on a bad faith claim. |

## 8. How coverage shapes the rest of the case

- **Demand strategy:** the demand is aimed at the limits that exist. If the injuries clearly exceed the limits, the attorney may send a **policy limits demand**, sometimes with a deadline. This needs the attorney's decision.
- **Releases:** a release with the at-fault insurer can end the case against that person. Read it with the attorney, and keep the UIM rights intact.
- **Liens and net recovery:** repayment rights of health plans, Medicare, Medicaid, providers and MedPay/PIP carriers reduce what the client keeps. Identify them early. They are the reason coverage and liens are always handled together.
- **Tracking:** record each insurer, adjuster, claim number, limits, coverage confirmation date, notices sent and deadlines, in one place in the case file.

## 9. Common mistakes to avoid

- Waiting for the other side to volunteer coverage information.
- Accepting a limits settlement before UIM notice and consent are handled.
- Forgetting an umbrella policy, an owner's policy or an employer's policy.
- Not opening the client's own MedPay or PIP claim early.
- Missing a short UM/UIM or notice-of-claim deadline.
- Relying on a verbal confirmation of limits.
- Treating a reservation of rights letter as routine.
- Overlooking rideshare status, workers' compensation or Medicare involvement.

## 10. Quick reference: coverage types

| Coverage | Whose policy | Pays for | Fault needed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| BI liability | At-fault party | Injuries to other people | Yes |
| PD liability | At-fault party | Damage to other people's property | Yes |
| UM / UIM | Client's | Injuries when the at-fault party has no or too little insurance | Yes (the other party's) |
| MedPay | Client's | Medical bills | No |
| PIP | Client's | Medical bills and some lost income | No |
| Collision | Client's | Client's own vehicle repair or replacement | No |
| Comprehensive | Client's | Theft, weather, vandalism, animals | No |
| Umbrella / excess | At-fault party's | Liability above the primary limits | Yes |
| Commercial / employer | Employer's | Injuries caused by an employee at work | Yes |
| Homeowners / CGL | Property owner's | Injuries on the property | Usually |
| Workers' compensation | Employer's | Work injuries | No |
